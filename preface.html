Before You Turn the Page

There are questions that deserve more than a passing response, conversations that require more than a few carefully selected words, and subjects that demand the courage to examine what we have believed, what we have taught, and how our beliefs have influenced the way we treat other people. This book begins with one such question, a question that reaches beyond denominational boundaries, challenges religious assumptions, and invites every reader to consider the responsibility that comes with representing Jesus Christ.

Who are you to call unclean?

Throughout the years, religious communities have developed language, traditions, expectations, and interpretations that influence how people understand holiness and their relationship with God. Some of these teachings have helped people grow in faith, develop spiritual discipline, and establish meaningful relationships with Christ. Others have become barriers through which people are evaluated, categorized, and sometimes rejected before anyone takes the time to understand their story. When religious convictions become the foundation for treating another human being as though they are unworthy of dignity, compassion, or fellowship, we must be willing to examine whether our conduct reflects the heart of the One we claim to follow.

Among the people who have experienced such rejection are members of the LGBTQIA+ community, many of whom have entered churches searching for spiritual connection, hoping to find encouragement, understanding, and a place where their faith could continue to grow. Some have encountered kindness, acceptance, and genuine friendship, while others have experienced public humiliation, family division, spiritual manipulation, and painful exclusion. Their experiences deserve to be heard rather than dismissed, and their humanity deserves to be recognized rather than reduced to a theological disagreement.

This book does not begin with an invitation to abandon Scripture, disregard holiness, or dismiss the importance of biblical interpretation. Rather, the invitation is to approach Scripture with humility, examine the context of the passages we quote, and consider whether our treatment of others reflects the compassion, mercy, and character of Jesus Christ. Faith should lead us toward a deeper understanding of God’s Word and a greater responsibility toward the people around us, especially those whose experiences differ from our own.

In Acts 10, Peter receives a vision that challenges his established understanding of what is clean and unclean. The vision becomes especially significant when Peter later explains that God has shown him that he should not call any person common or unclean. The account involving Cornelius and his household reveals a moment of profound change in the early church, as Peter begins to understand the implications of God’s work beyond the boundaries he had previously recognized. The passage deserves careful attention, not because one verse answers every modern theological question, but because the entire account challenges readers to examine the assumptions they bring into their relationships with other people.

Religious certainty can become dangerous when humility disappears. A person may know Scripture, understand church traditions, participate in ministry, and maintain a reputation for spiritual commitment while overlooking the responsibility to treat others with dignity. Knowledge without compassion can leave wounds, authority without accountability can create fear, and conviction without humility can turn a place of worship into a place where people feel they must defend their worth before they are permitted to belong.

The chapters ahead will examine religious labels, inherited traditions, spiritual authority, Peter’s vision, the experiences of LGBTQIA+ people, the example of Jesus, and the responsibility Christians carry when speaking about holiness and belonging. These conversations will not always be comfortable, especially for readers who have never questioned the assumptions they inherited or considered how those assumptions may have affected someone else’s life. Nevertheless, meaningful spiritual growth requires a willingness to listen, reconsider, study, and allow our understanding to mature.

My hope is that readers will approach these pages with open hearts and Bibles ready for examination. Those who have experienced rejection may find encouragement in knowing that their stories deserve to be heard, while those who have participated in excluding others may discover an opportunity to reconsider how their faith is expressed. Pastors, ministry leaders, teachers, parents, and everyday believers may also find reasons to examine the influence their words and decisions have upon people who are searching for a place to experience God’s love.

Before turning the page, consider one question: Have you ever assigned a label to someone without understanding their story, their faith, or their relationship with God? Have you ever mistaken your personal discomfort for a biblical declaration, or allowed a familiar tradition to become more influential than the responsibility to love your neighbor?

The purpose of this book is not to place one group of people on trial while declaring another group innocent. The purpose is to invite every reader into a deeper examination of faith, interpretation, compassion, and personal responsibility. We cannot speak confidently about the character of Jesus while refusing to examine the character our own actions reveal.

The question remains before us, and every chapter will bring us closer to understanding why the question matters.

Who are you to call unclean what God has made holy?
