Chapter 5

What God Has Cleansed

Peter's arrival at Cornelius's home brings the vision down from the rooftop and into a room full of people. The revelation is no longer something to ponder privately. Peter must respond to the household gathered before him. The people waiting for him are not a theoretical question. They have names, prayers, expectations, and a story of how God directed them to this moment.

Peter acknowledges the unusual nature of his visit. He explains that God has shown him not to call any person common or unclean. The words connect his understanding directly to the people in front of him. He has not merely reconsidered a symbol. His response has changed the way he enters a relationship and recognizes who may hear the message of Christ.

“God hath shewed me that I should not call any man common or unclean.”

Acts 10:28

Peter's statement is significant because he speaks about people. The vision prepared him for this household, and now he understands why he was sent. He had crossed a boundary he previously believed he should maintain. His willingness to enter the home creates the setting for a conversation that could not have happened while he remained outside.

Cornelius tells Peter why he sent for him. He describes the direction he received and explains that the household has gathered to hear what God has commanded. Their testimony is part of the moment. Peter has come to listen as well as speak.

Imagine the significance of Peter standing in that home. The encounter is not about Peter congratulating himself for being willing to cross a boundary. God had already been working in Cornelius's life. Peter's task was to recognize that work and respond faithfully. The people in the room were not waiting for Peter to make them worthy of attention. They had gathered in obedience to what they believed God had said.

Peter begins proclaiming Jesus. He speaks of Christ's ministry, death, resurrection, and the forgiveness offered through Him. He does not make the gathering about his former reluctance or turn the moment into an explanation of why he had kept his distance. The message directs attention toward the Lord.

“God is no respecter of persons: But in every nation he that feareth him, and worketh righteousness, is accepted with him.”

Acts 10:34–35

Peter's declaration emerges from what he is witnessing. The passage describes God's work among people from another nation, and the significance rests within that setting. The church must preserve that context while also taking seriously what Peter says about the way God regards people and the limits of human distinctions.

Peter's message is not finished when the Holy Spirit falls upon those listening. The believers who accompanied him are astonished. Peter recognizes the evidence before him and asks whether anyone can forbid baptism to those who have received the Spirit as they had. He then directs that they be baptized.

The change in Peter is visible through his actions. He entered the home, listened, proclaimed Christ, recognized God's work, and responded. His response is not simply a revised opinion. The encounter changed whom he was willing to receive and how he understood the movement of God's work beyond familiar boundaries.

For readers who have experienced religious rejection, the story may raise deeply personal questions. What happens when people decide your presence is a problem before hearing you? What does it mean when someone approaches you only to manage or correct you? Cornelius's household reminds us that people are not merely waiting to be evaluated. They have lives, prayers, experiences, and a relationship with God that deserves to be heard with care.

Acts 10 specifically describes the inclusion of Gentiles and God's work among them. The passage should not be used to flatten every theological question into one easy conclusion. Nevertheless, the story confronts the church with a clear responsibility: human boundaries do not tell us everything about where God is working, and we must remain willing to recognize His activity when it challenges our expectations.

Cornelius's testimony was not an interruption to Peter's revelation. Listening helped Peter understand why he had been sent. A church should make room for people to tell their stories without treating those stories as distractions from spiritual matters. Listening may reveal circumstances we did not know, pain we did not recognize, or evidence of God's work we had not considered.

The story moves from revelation to relationship, from instruction to action. Peter did not remain outside discussing what might be happening. He entered, heard, witnessed, and responded. That is the movement this chapter leaves with us. When our understanding is challenged, the next step may require us to meet someone rather than merely debate them.

Recognition brings responsibility. Once a person sees that God is working beyond the boundaries they expected, they must consider what their position, influence, or leadership will do next. The next chapter turns toward those who hold spiritual authority and asks whether they are serving people or standing in the doorway as gatekeepers.
