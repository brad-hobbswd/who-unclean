Chapter 5

What God Has Cleansed

The words spoken in Peter’s vision were direct (Acts 10:15): “What God hath cleansed, that call not thou common.” Peter had been raised with traditions that distinguished between clean and unclean, acceptable and unacceptable. Those distinctions were deeply woven into his understanding of faith, obedience, and the people with whom he could associate. The voice from heaven challenged something Peter had believed for years, not through an argument from another man, but through a revelation that demanded a response.

On the morrow, as they went on their journey, and drew nigh unto the city, Peter went up upon the housetop to pray about the sixth hour: And he became very hungry, and would have eaten: but while they made ready, he fell into a trance, And saw heaven opened, and a certain vessel descending upon him, as it had been a great sheet knit at the four corners, and let down to the earth: Wherein were all manner of fourfooted beasts of the earth, and wild beasts, and creeping things, and fowls of the air. And there came a voice to him, Rise, Peter; kill, and eat. But Peter said, Not so, Lord; for I have never eaten any thing that is common or unclean. And the voice spake unto him again the second time, What God hath cleansed, that call not thou common. This was done thrice: and the vessel was received up again into heaven.

Acts 10:9–16

Now while Peter doubted in himself what this vision which he had seen should mean, behold, men which were sent from Cornelius had made inquiry for Simon’s house, and stood before the gate, And called, and asked whether Simon, which was surnamed Peter, were lodged there. While Peter thought on the vision, the Spirit said unto him, Behold, three men seek thee. Arise therefore, and get thee down, and go with them, doubting nothing: for I have sent them. Then Peter went down to the men which were sent unto him from Cornelius; and said, Behold, I am he whom ye seek: what is the cause wherefore ye are come? And they said, Cornelius the centurion, a just man, and one that feareth God, and of good report among all the nation of the Jews, was warned from God by an holy angel to send for thee into his house, and to hear words of thee. Then called he them in, and lodged them. And on the morrow Peter went away with them, and certain brethren from Joppa accompanied him.

Acts 10:17–23

And he said unto them, Ye know how that it is an unlawful thing for a man that is a Jew to keep company, or come unto one of another nation; but God hath shewed me that I should not call any man common or unclean.

Acts 10:28

Therefore came I unto you without gainsaying, as soon as I was sent for: I ask therefore for what intent ye have sent for me? And Cornelius said, Four days ago I was fasting until this hour; and at the ninth hour I prayed in my house, and, behold, a man stood before me in bright clothing, And said, Cornelius, thy prayer is heard, and thine alms are had in remembrance in the sight of God. Send therefore to Joppa, and call hither Simon, whose surname is Peter; he is lodged in the house of one Simon a tanner by the sea side: who, when he cometh, shall speak unto thee. Immediately therefore I sent to thee; and thou hast well done that thou art come. Now therefore are we all here present before God, to hear all things that are commanded thee of God.

Acts 10:29–33

That distinction matters when discussing Scripture today. The passage does not invite us to invent a new gospel or dismiss every biblical teaching that challenges our preferences. The passage does, however, confront the danger of declaring people unacceptable through assumptions that God has not authorized us to make. Peter had to distinguish between what God had commanded and what Peter had come to believe through his upbringing. Such examination remains necessary for the church.

Peter’s explanation is followed by Cornelius describing why he sent for him. The account makes clear that the vision, the Spirit’s direction, and the testimony of Cornelius work together in Peter’s understanding. The teaching should therefore be drawn from the full encounter rather than from the vision’s phrase alone.

People have carried religious declarations for years that were spoken over them without careful consideration. Some were told that God could never use them, that their presence would contaminate a congregation, that their prayers were unwelcome, or that their lives placed them permanently outside the reach of grace. LGBTQIA+ individuals have heard such declarations from pulpits, classrooms, family members, and church communities. Those words can leave wounds far deeper than the person speaking may ever understand.

The church must be careful when speaking about cleansing, holiness, and acceptance. Scripture teaches that God is holy, that humanity needs redemption, and that transformation belongs to the work of God. Those truths should never become excuses for treating another human being as though dignity, compassion, or access to the presence of God must be withheld. The gospel calls people toward God. The gospel does not give human beings permission to take God’s place as the final authority over another person’s worth.

Peter did not understand everything in one moment. His revelation unfolded through obedience, conversation, observation, and the unmistakable work of the Holy Ghost. The people he once viewed through a restricted understanding were standing before him, and God was moving among them. Peter could no longer rely upon his former assumptions without confronting what God was doing in front of him.

Perhaps the church needs the same humility. A person may have spent decades defending a particular interpretation, yet the willingness to examine Scripture honestly remains a sign of spiritual maturity rather than weakness. God does not lose His authority when someone admits the need for deeper understanding. Truth does not become fragile through sincere examination.

The question is not whether we possess convictions. The question is whether our convictions are genuinely submitted to God. Are we allowing Scripture to shape our understanding, or are we forcing every person we encounter into a framework we have never been willing to examine? Are we listening when God challenges our assumptions, or are we answering with Peter’s initial words, “Not so, Lord,” before we have fully understood what the Lord is showing us?

Peter eventually entered the house, spoke with Cornelius, and witnessed the Spirit of God fall upon people (Acts 10:34–48) he had previously considered outside the boundaries of his understanding. His obedience opened a door that tradition had kept closed. The lesson was not that Peter should stop honoring God. The lesson was that Peter must never allow his own understanding to become greater than God’s ability to reveal, redeem, and receive.

Then Peter opened his mouth, and said, Of a truth I perceive that God is no respecter of persons: But in every nation he that feareth him, and worketh righteousness, is accepted with him.

Acts 10:34–35

While Peter yet spake these words, the Holy Ghost fell on all them which heard the word. And they of the circumcision which believed were astonished, as many as came with Peter, because that on the Gentiles also was poured out the gift of the Holy Ghost. For they heard them speak with tongues, and magnify God. Then answered Peter, Can any man forbid water, that these should not be baptized, which have received the Holy Ghost as well as we? And he commanded them to be baptized in the name of the Lord. Then prayed they him to tarry certain days.

Acts 10:44–48

What God has cleansed should not be treated as common merely because someone has difficulty understanding the work of God. The church must remain humble enough to listen, courageous enough to examine its traditions, and compassionate enough to recognize the human beings standing before us. God remains the One who calls, cleanses, saves, and transforms. Our responsibility is to follow Him rather than place ourselves in His seat.
