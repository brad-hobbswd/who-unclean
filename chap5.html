Chapter 5

What God Has Cleansed

Peter's arrival at Cornelius's home marks a change that began on the rooftop but could not be completed there. He has come because God directed him, yet the meaning of that direction becomes clearer through the people gathered in the house. The encounter is no longer a vision to consider privately. Peter is standing among people whose lives, prayers, and expectations are now part of the revelation he must understand.

Peter acknowledges the unusual nature of his visit. He explains that God has shown him not to call any person common or unclean. The words matter because Peter connects his understanding directly to the people in front of him. He has not merely reconsidered a symbol. His response has changed the way he enters a relationship and the way he recognizes who may hear the message of Christ.

Acts 10:28

Cornelius then tells Peter why he sent for him. He describes the direction he received and explains that the household has gathered to hear what God has commanded. The room is filled with people waiting, not for a debate about whether they are worthy to be there, but to hear the message Peter has been sent to share. Their testimony gives Peter a fuller understanding of the circumstances that brought him to this moment.

Acts 10:29–33

Imagine what that moment may have meant to the people in Cornelius's home. They had gathered because they believed God had directed them. Peter, a Jewish disciple of Jesus, was now standing among them. His presence carried the significance of a boundary being crossed, but the encounter was not about Peter making himself generous or deciding to tolerate them. God had already been at work in Cornelius's life. Peter was arriving to recognize and respond to that work.

Peter begins speaking about Jesus. He does not make himself the center of the story, nor does he turn the gathering into an explanation of why he had previously kept his distance. He proclaims the life, ministry, death, and resurrection of Christ. The message is the reason they have gathered, and Peter's words direct their attention toward the Lord rather than toward the history of the separation between them.

Acts 10:34–43

Peter's declaration that God is no respecter of persons emerges from the encounter. He is recognizing something through the circumstances unfolding before him. The passage describes God's work among people from another nation, and the significance rests within that setting. The church must preserve that context while also taking seriously what Peter says about the way God regards people and the limits of human distinctions.

The story does not end with Peter finishing his message. The Holy Spirit falls upon those listening. The believers who accompanied Peter are astonished, and Peter recognizes the evidence before him. He asks whether anyone can forbid baptism to those who have received the Holy Spirit as they had. The question is not theoretical. The people in that room have experienced the work of God, and Peter responds to what he has witnessed.

Acts 10:44–48

The change in Peter is visible through his actions. He came into the home, listened, preached, recognized God's work, and instructed that those who received the Spirit be baptized. His response is not simply a revised opinion. The encounter changed what he was willing to do, whom he was willing to receive, and how he understood the movement of God's work beyond familiar boundaries.

For readers who have experienced religious rejection, the story may raise deeply personal questions. What happens when people have already decided that your presence is a problem before they have listened to your testimony? What does it mean when someone approaches you not because they are curious about your life, but because they believe they must manage or correct you? Cornelius's household reminds us that people are not merely waiting to be evaluated. They have lives, prayers, experiences, and a relationship with God that deserves to be heard with care.

The passage must not be used to flatten every theological question into one easy conclusion. Acts 10 is specifically describing the inclusion of Gentiles and the recognition of God's work among them. The wider application requires thoughtful interpretation. Nevertheless, the story confronts the church with a clear responsibility: we must not assume that our boundaries tell us everything about where God is working, and we must remain willing to recognize His activity when it challenges our expectations.

Peter's experience also offers a lesson about listening. Cornelius's testimony was not an interruption to the revelation. His account helped Peter understand why he had been sent. The church should make room for people to tell their stories without treating those stories as distractions from spiritual matters. Listening may reveal circumstances we did not know, pain we did not recognize, or evidence of God's work we had not considered.

What God had shown Peter became meaningful through the people he encountered and the response he made. The story moves from revelation to relationship, from instruction to action. Peter did not remain outside the home discussing what might be happening. He entered, heard, witnessed, and responded. That is the movement this chapter leaves with us: when our understanding is challenged, the next step may require us to meet someone rather than merely debate them.

The encounter brings us to another question. Once a person recognizes that God is working beyond the boundaries they expected, what responsibility comes with that recognition? Who should be helping people approach God, and what happens when those entrusted with spiritual care begin controlling access instead?

Therefore came I unto you without gainsaying, as soon as I was sent for: I ask therefore for what intent ye have sent for me? And Cornelius said, Four days ago I was fasting until this hour; and at the ninth hour I prayed in my house, and, behold, a man stood before me in bright clothing, And said, Cornelius, thy prayer is heard, and thine alms are had in remembrance in the sight of God. Acts 10:34–35

The word which God sent unto the children of Israel, preaching peace by Jesus Christ (he is Lord of all): That word, I say, which was published throughout all Judaea, and began from Galilee, after the baptism which John preached; How God anointed Jesus of Nazareth with the Holy Ghost and with power: who went about doing good, and healing all that were oppressed of the devil; for God was with him. And we are witnesses of all things which he did both in the land of the Jews, and in Jerusalem; whom they slew and hanged on a tree: Him God raised up the third day, and shewed him openly; Not to all the people, but unto witnesses chosen before of God, even to us, who did eat and drink with him after he rose from the dead. And he commanded us to preach unto the people, and to testify that he is he which was ordained of God to be the Judge of quick and dead. To him give all the prophets witness, that through his name whosoever believeth in him shall receive remission of sins.

Acts 10:36–43

While Peter yet spake these words, the Holy Ghost fell on all them which heard the word. And they of the circumcision which believed were astonished, as many as came with Peter, because that on the Gentiles also was poured out the gift of the Holy Ghost. For they heard them speak with tongues, and magnify God. Then answered Peter, Can any man forbid water, that these should not be baptized, which have received the Holy Ghost as well as we? And he commanded them to be baptized in the name of the Lord. Then prayed they him to tarry certain days.
