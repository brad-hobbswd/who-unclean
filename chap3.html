Chapter 3

When Tradition Becomes a Wall

Tradition can provide a sense of stability, belonging, and continuity, especially within communities built around shared faith and longstanding practices. Many people find comfort in familiar ways of worship, established teachings, and the experiences of generations who came before them. Traditions can remind us of our spiritual heritage and encourage us to remain connected to the values that shaped our faith. Nevertheless, tradition becomes a problem when familiarity is mistaken for unquestionable authority or when practices established by people begin preventing others from experiencing the grace, compassion, and truth of God. A tradition may have been meaningful in one season of the church’s history without necessarily becoming a permanent measure by which every person must be evaluated.

The difficulty often arises when people no longer distinguish between the teachings of Scripture and the customs developed around those teachings. A practice may become so familiar that questioning the practice feels equivalent to questioning God, even when the practice itself was never established as a biblical command. Over time, people may begin defending traditions without examining their origins, purposes, or consequences, allowing inherited expectations to influence decisions more strongly than the principles they were intended to support. Such patterns can create an environment where sincere questions are discouraged, unfamiliar perspectives are treated as threats, and spiritual growth becomes difficult because people fear challenging what has always been accepted.

Jesus confronted this issue during His ministry when religious leaders placed considerable emphasis on traditions that influenced how they understood obedience. In Mark 7:5–13, He addressed the difference between commandments of God and traditions maintained by people, explaining that religious customs could become so influential that they displaced the very responsibilities Scripture established. His concern was not that every tradition lacked value, but that people could elevate human practices to a position where those practices interfered with obedience to God. When tradition becomes the standard by which Scripture is interpreted rather than something examined through Scripture, the purpose of faith can become distorted.

5 Then the Pharisees and scribes asked him, Why walk not thy disciples according to the tradition of the elders, but eat bread with unwashen hands?

6 He answered and said unto them, Well hath Esaias prophesied of you hypocrites, as it is written, This people honoureth me with their lips, but their heart is far from me.

7 Howbeit in vain do they worship me, teaching for doctrines the commandments of men.

8 For laying aside the commandment of God, ye hold the tradition of men, as the washing of pots and cups: and many other such like things ye do.

9 And he said unto them, Full well ye reject the commandment of God, that ye may keep your own tradition.

10 For Moses said, Honour thy father and thy mother; and, Whoso curseth father or mother, let him die the death:

11 But ye say, If a man shall say to his father or mother, It is Corban, that is to say, a gift, by whatsoever thou mightest be profited by me; he shall be free.

12 And ye suffer him no more to do ought for his father or his mother;

13 Making the word of God of none effect through your tradition, which ye have delivered: and many such like things do ye.

Mark 7:5–13

This distinction remains important within modern churches because traditions often shape how people are welcomed, how ministry opportunities are distributed, and how congregations respond to circumstances they have not previously encountered. Some expectations may be rooted in careful biblical teaching, while others may reflect cultural habits, personal preferences, or interpretations passed down without much examination. When those distinctions are ignored, people may be judged according to standards that carry significant influence within a congregation but lack the biblical foundation their advocates assume they possess. The responsibility to examine such practices belongs to every generation of believers, particularly those who teach, lead, and influence others.

The consequences can be especially painful for people whose experiences do not fit comfortably within established religious expectations. Members of the LGBTQIA+ community may encounter traditions that determine how they are permitted to participate, whether their relationships are acknowledged, or how openly they may discuss their experiences within a church. In some communities, these expectations have developed through interpretations that are rarely examined beyond the boundaries of the congregation itself. Individuals may discover that the treatment they receive is influenced not only by Scripture but also by cultural assumptions and traditions that have become difficult to distinguish from biblical instruction. When those assumptions are presented as unquestionable declarations from God, people may feel that challenging the practice requires challenging their faith.

A wall built from tradition can remain standing even when the people who constructed it are no longer present. The language may change, the congregation may grow, and the community may become more diverse, yet the underlying expectations continue shaping relationships and decisions. People who question those expectations may be regarded as disruptive simply because they are asking whether the practice still serves its intended purpose. Such reactions can prevent meaningful conversations and discourage individuals from examining Scripture honestly, particularly when the consequences of asking questions include losing relationships, ministry opportunities, or a sense of belonging.

The New Testament provides examples of the early church confronting traditions and assumptions that affected its understanding of who could participate in the community of faith. The arrival of Gentile believers challenged established expectations concerning religious identity, fellowship, and the boundaries of God’s people. These conversations were not resolved simply by maintaining the practices that had been familiar to previous generations. The apostles had to examine what God was doing, consider the testimony of those involved, and recognize how the work of the Holy Spirit was influencing their understanding. Their experiences demonstrate that spiritual growth sometimes requires people to reconsider conclusions they once believed were beyond question.

Tradition becomes particularly difficult to examine when it is connected to personal identity or spiritual reputation. Leaders may fear that reconsidering an established practice will be interpreted as abandoning their convictions, while members may worry that questioning familiar teachings will create conflict within their families or congregations. Those concerns can discourage honest study and make it easier to preserve an existing position than to examine whether the position accurately reflects Scripture. Nevertheless, the responsibility to seek truth remains important, and faith should not depend upon avoiding questions that deserve careful consideration.

The church must be willing to recognize that maintaining a tradition and demonstrating faithfulness to God are not always identical responsibilities. A practice may continue because people are accustomed to it, because changing it would require difficult conversations, or because the community has never considered another approach. Such circumstances do not automatically establish that the practice is wrong, but they do reveal why examination is necessary. Believers should be able to explain the biblical foundation for their decisions rather than relying solely upon the fact that a particular expectation has existed for many years.

This examination should be approached with humility rather than hostility toward those who value tradition. Many people have sincere reasons for maintaining practices that have shaped their spiritual lives, and meaningful conversations require respect for the experiences and convictions of everyone involved. The purpose is not to dismiss the past but to determine whether the traditions we maintain continue reflecting the principles of Scripture. A church that values truth should be willing to examine its practices without assuming that every question represents rebellion or that every change represents compromise.

When tradition becomes a wall, the people standing on either side may begin viewing one another as opponents rather than members of the same human family. Conversations become defensive, relationships become strained, and opportunities for understanding diminish because everyone feels responsible for protecting a position. Such divisions can be especially difficult when people are already navigating questions of faith, identity, family relationships, and belonging. The church should recognize the seriousness of these circumstances and approach them with the patience required to listen, study, and respond thoughtfully.

Faithfulness to God requires more than preserving familiar practices. It requires a willingness to examine our understanding, recognize the influence of our experiences, and remain accountable to Scripture. Traditions may serve the church well when they encourage worship, spiritual growth, fellowship, and a deeper understanding of God, but they should never become barriers that prevent believers from recognizing the work of God in people’s lives. The responsibility to preserve what is meaningful must always be accompanied by the responsibility to examine what may have become unnecessarily restrictive.

The question is not whether tradition has a place within the church. The question is whether we have allowed tradition to occupy a place that belongs to God alone. When familiar practices become more influential than Scripture, when established expectations prevent us from listening, and when people are excluded because their lives challenge assumptions we have never examined, we must be willing to reconsider the walls we have built.

God’s work is not limited by our familiarity, and our understanding should never become so rigid that we cannot recognize His movement beyond the boundaries we have established. The church must remain willing to examine its traditions, not because every tradition is harmful, but because every tradition must remain accountable to the God it claims to honor.
