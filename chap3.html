Chapter 3

When Tradition Becomes a Wall

Traditions often begin with sincere devotion. A familiar practice may remind a congregation of its history, help people worship, or preserve something meaningful across generations. People may associate a tradition with family, spiritual beginnings, or a moment when God met them. Those memories deserve respect. Trouble begins when the practice becomes so familiar that questioning the custom feels like questioning God.

There is a difference between what Scripture teaches and the habits people build around that teaching. The two may be connected, but they are not automatically identical. A tradition may be wise without being a command. A standard may serve one setting well and require reconsideration in another. Longevity alone cannot establish whether a practice is being applied fairly or whether the practice still serves the purpose people believe it serves.

In Mark 7, religious leaders question Jesus because His disciples do not follow a customary washing practice. The conversation moves beyond the immediate question of hands and meals. Jesus confronts the danger of placing human tradition above the instruction and purpose of God.

“This people honoureth me with their lips, but their heart is far from me.”

Mark 7:6

Jesus' words are not a blanket condemnation of every inherited practice. He is exposing what happens when outward observance becomes a substitute for obedience and when people defend their customs while neglecting what God has actually required. A practice can look religious and still be used to avoid a more difficult responsibility.

That distinction matters when traditions influence who is welcomed, whose questions are permitted, which relationships are acknowledged, or whose experiences may be discussed openly. People may follow expectations without realizing those expectations contain a mixture of biblical interpretation, culture, and institutional habit. Sometimes the practice has a clear foundation. Sometimes the explanation rests largely on what a community has always done.

Someone outside those expectations may hear a human practice described as God's requirement. The difference can be painful. A person may believe that questioning the custom means questioning God Himself, even when the community has never made room to examine the custom's foundation. Spiritual certainty can become especially heavy when the person carrying the burden has no opportunity to ask how the conclusion was reached.

Consider a congregation that has long discouraged questions about a particular subject. The practice may have begun with a desire to protect unity. Over time, the silence becomes an unwritten rule. A young adult asks an honest question, and instead of discussing the Scripture or the reasoning behind the rule, the conversation turns toward the young person's motives. The question is treated as evidence of rebellion. The community has protected the custom from examination by making the person asking the question feel unsafe.

Questions can unsettle people whose sense of identity or security is tied to familiar conclusions. A leader may fear losing trust. A family member may worry about disappointing loved ones. Someone who has already experienced rejection may fear another loss. Those fears deserve to be heard rather than used to shut down examination.

Examining tradition is not about dismissing everything we inherited. Many traditions have helped people worship, serve, remember, and remain connected to faith. The task is to distinguish what the text says, how we have understood the text, and what our practices have produced in people's lives. Respect for spiritual heritage and humility before God can coexist.

Sometimes a wall is visible. More often, the wall is built from subjects no one discusses, questions people learn not to ask, and assumptions defended simply because they are familiar. Good intentions do not remove the need to examine the results of our practices, especially when others are living with consequences we may never experience ourselves.

When a practice cannot be explained from Scripture without relying on “we have always done this,” we should be willing to pause. When a custom prevents us from listening, we should ask whether the custom has been given authority beyond its proper place. When our interpretation is challenged, we should be able to study without assuming that every question is an attack.

The next chapter follows Peter, a sincere disciple whose understanding was challenged while he was praying. He was not searching for a reason to abandon his faith. God interrupted his familiar understanding and led him toward an encounter that would require him to reconsider what he believed about people and fellowship.
