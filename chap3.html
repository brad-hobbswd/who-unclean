Chapter 3

When Tradition Becomes a Wall

Traditions are often born from sincere devotion. A familiar way of worship, a practice passed through generations, or a standard that once helped a community remain faithful can carry deep meaning. People may associate such practices with their families, their spiritual beginnings, and moments when God met them. The value of that history should not be dismissed. The question arises when a practice becomes so familiar that no one remembers why it began, or when the custom is treated as though questioning the custom were the same as questioning God.

There is a difference between what Scripture teaches and what a community has learned to do around that teaching. The two may be connected, but they are not automatically identical. A practice can reflect wisdom without being a command. An expectation can be helpful in one setting and unnecessary in another. A tradition can serve a congregation well for years and still require examination when circumstances change or when the practice begins producing consequences its founders never intended.

Jesus addressed this distinction in Mark 7. The religious leaders questioned His disciples over a practice associated with the traditions of the elders. Jesus directed the conversation toward a deeper problem: human tradition can become so elevated that people neglect the responsibilities God has actually given them. The concern was not that every inherited practice was meaningless. The concern was that tradition could be defended while the heart of God's instruction was being set aside.

The passage gives us a serious reason to examine what we have inherited. Familiarity can make an expectation feel sacred before we have considered its foundation. A person may defend a practice with great confidence simply because the practice has always been present in their church. Yet longevity alone does not answer whether a tradition accurately reflects Scripture, whether the tradition is being applied fairly, or whether the practice is accomplishing the purpose people believe it serves.

This examination is not an invitation to treat every tradition with suspicion. Some practices help people worship, remember, serve, and remain connected to their faith. The people who cherish them may have meaningful experiences attached to those practices. A thoughtful conversation should honor those experiences while also asking whether the tradition has been given authority beyond its proper place.

The difficulty becomes more pronounced when a tradition determines how people are received. A congregation may have developed expectations about who can participate, what questions may be asked, which relationships are acknowledged, or whose experiences are considered acceptable to discuss. Those expectations can become so embedded that people follow them without recognizing that the rules may reflect a mixture of biblical interpretation, cultural assumptions, and institutional habit.

For someone who finds themselves outside those expectations, the distinction may be consequential. They may hear a practice described as God's requirement when the explanation actually rests upon an interpretation or custom that has never been openly examined. The person is then left believing that disagreement with the practice means disagreement with God Himself. That is a heavy burden to place upon anyone, particularly when the community has not made room for careful questions or honest conversation.

The LGBTQIA+ discussion is one area where inherited expectations can shape the life of a congregation. People may have learned certain conclusions through family, church culture, or the voices of trusted leaders. Those conclusions can influence the way someone is welcomed, whether their relationships are acknowledged, and whether they feel safe speaking honestly. Examining a tradition does not require us to presume the answer before study begins. It requires us to distinguish what the text says, how we have interpreted the text, and how those interpretations have been lived out in the lives of real people.

Questions can feel threatening when a person's identity, reputation, or sense of spiritual security is tied to a familiar position. A leader may fear losing trust. A member may worry about disappointing family. Someone who has been rejected may fear that speaking honestly will lead to another loss. Such fears can keep a community from doing the work of examination, even when the questions are sincere and deserve attention.

A healthy examination requires more than winning an argument. It asks us to listen carefully, read responsibly, acknowledge what we know and what we do not know, and remain willing to revise conclusions when the evidence calls for deeper understanding. Respect for tradition and humility before Scripture can coexist. We do not honor our spiritual heritage by refusing to examine the ways that heritage has shaped us.

The walls built by tradition are not always visible. Sometimes they appear in the subjects people avoid, the questions that are discouraged, or the quiet assumptions about who is likely to belong. Sometimes the wall is defended by people who sincerely believe they are protecting the church. Good intentions do not remove the need to examine the results of our practices, especially when others are experiencing the consequences.

The question is not whether a tradition is old or new. The question is whether we have allowed a human practice to carry authority that belongs to God. When we cannot explain the biblical foundation for an expectation, when our custom prevents us from listening, or when we refuse to reconsider a practice simply because it is familiar, we should pause and examine the wall before insisting that everyone remain on one side of it.

The next chapter brings this question into the life of Peter. He was not looking for a reason to discard his faith. He was praying, living within the understanding he had received, and then God interrupted that familiar understanding. Peter's experience shows what can happen when a sincere believer is confronted with something that cannot be comfortably explained by what he has always known.

Then the Pharisees and scribes asked him, Why walk not thy disciples according to the tradition of the elders, but eat bread with unwashen hands? He answered and said unto them, Well hath Esaias prophesied of you hypocrites, as it is written, This people honoureth me with their lips, but their heart is far from me.

Howbeit in vain do they worship me, teaching for doctrines the commandments of men. For laying aside the commandment of God, ye hold the tradition of men, as the washing of pots and cups: and many other such like things ye do.

And he said unto them, Full well ye reject the commandment of God, that ye may keep your own tradition. For Moses said, Honour thy father and thy mother; and, Whoso curseth father or mother, let him die the death: But ye say, If a man shall say to his father or mother, It is Corban, that is to say, a gift, by whatsoever thou mightest be profited by me; he shall be free.

And ye suffer him no more to do ought for his father or his mother; Making the word of God of none effect through your tradition, which ye have delivered: and many such like things do ye.

Mark 7:5–13
