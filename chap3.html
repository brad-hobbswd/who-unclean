Chapter 3

When Tradition Becomes a Wall

Traditions often begin with sincere devotion. A familiar practice may remind a congregation of its history, help people worship, or preserve something meaningful across generations. Those memories deserve respect. Trouble begins when the practice becomes so familiar that questioning the custom feels like questioning God.

Scripture and the habits built around Scripture are not automatically the same thing. A tradition may be wise without being a command. A standard may serve one setting well and need reconsideration in another. Longevity alone cannot establish whether a practice is being applied fairly or whether the practice still serves the purpose people believe it serves.

In Mark 7, Jesus challenges religious leaders who had elevated human tradition while neglecting God's instruction. His concern was not that every inherited practice was worthless. His warning was that people could defend a custom so fiercely that the heart of their faith was overlooked.

That distinction matters when traditions influence who is welcomed, whose questions are permitted, which relationships are acknowledged, or whose experiences may be discussed openly. People may follow expectations without realizing those expectations contain a mixture of biblical interpretation, culture, and institutional habit.

Someone outside those expectations may hear a human practice described as God's requirement. The difference can be painful. A person may believe that questioning the custom means questioning God, even when the community has never made room to examine the custom's foundation.

Questions can unsettle people whose sense of identity or security is tied to familiar conclusions. A leader may fear losing trust. A family member may worry about disappointing loved ones. Someone who has already experienced rejection may fear another loss. Those fears deserve to be heard rather than used to shut down examination.

Examining tradition is not about dismissing everything we inherited. The task is to distinguish what the text says, how we have understood the text, and what our practices have produced in people's lives. Respect for spiritual heritage and humility before God can coexist.

Sometimes a wall is visible. More often, the wall is built from subjects no one discusses, questions people learn not to ask, and assumptions defended simply because they are familiar. The next chapter follows Peter, a sincere disciple whose understanding was challenged while he was praying. His story reminds us that a person can love God and still have more to learn.

Then the Pharisees and scribes asked him, Why walk not thy disciples according to the tradition of the elders, but eat bread with unwashen hands? He answered and said unto them, Well hath Esaias prophesied of you hypocrites, as it is written, This people honoureth me with their lips, but their heart is far from me.

Howbeit in vain do they worship me, teaching for doctrines the commandments of men. For laying aside the commandment of God, ye hold the tradition of men, as the washing of pots and cups: and many other such like things ye do.

And he said unto them, Full well ye reject the commandment of God, that ye may keep your own tradition. For Moses said, Honour thy father and thy mother; and, Whoso curseth father or mother, let him die the death: But ye say, If a man shall say to his father or mother, It is Corban, that is to say, a gift, by whatsoever thou mightest be profited by me; he shall be free.

And ye suffer him no more to do ought for his father or his mother; Making the word of God of none effect through your tradition, which ye have delivered: and many such like things do ye.

Mark 7:5–13
