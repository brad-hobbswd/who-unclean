Chapter 2

The Problem With Religious Labels

A label rarely stays between two people. In a congregation, a reputation can travel ahead of someone and quietly determine who trusts them, who invites them to sit nearby, and whose questions receive patience. A description becomes an assumption, and the assumption begins shaping ordinary behavior.

Consider a visitor who arrives after hearing that a particular member is “trouble.” No one says anything openly, yet conversations become guarded and invitations never come. The visitor may not know what has been said. They only feel the room change around them. The community may call the behavior caution without ever checking whether the story was accurate or complete.

Luke describes Jesus at Levi's table with tax collectors and others who drew criticism. The religious observers questioned His choice of company. Jesus answered by explaining His purpose: He came to call sinners. The gathering was not disqualified from spiritual significance by the reputations of those present.

James addresses another form of sorting people, this time by wealth and appearance. His example exposes favoritism within a gathering of believers. The problem was more than poor manners. The community's treatment of people contradicted the faith they professed.

We can see the same pattern when someone is known chiefly by one part of their life. LGBTQIA+ people may find that identity becomes the only subject others discuss, while their work, family, faith, friendships, grief, talents, and ordinary joys remain unseen. They can become the subject of a conversation without ever being invited into the room where conclusions are made.

Being repeatedly defined by others carries a cost. A person may rehearse what to disclose, avoid certain conversations, or feel responsible for correcting every assumption. The burden grows when belonging seems conditional upon silence. Listening does not require everyone to reach the same conclusion; listening refuses to treat a person's humanity as secondary.

A congregation's welcome is measured in the small distinctions members make. Who is given time to explain? Who receives compassion after a mistake? Who is believed? Who is left standing alone? Our answers reveal the culture more honestly than a statement printed on a wall.

Labels can help describe a concern, but they should never become a substitute for knowing a person. The next question is where some of our assumptions came from. Traditions can carry wisdom and meaning, yet a familiar practice may also be mistaken for a command simply because no one has examined the difference.

My brethren, have not the faith of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Lord of glory, with respect of persons. For if there come unto your assembly a man with a gold ring, in goodly apparel, and there come in also a poor man in vile raiment; And ye have respect to him that wear the gay clothing, and say unto him, Sit thou here in a good place; and say to the poor, Stand thou there, or sit here under my footstool: Are ye not then partial in yourselves, and are become judges of evil thoughts?

Hearken, my beloved brethren, Hath not God chosen the poor of this world rich in faith, and heirs of the kingdom which he hath promised to them that love him? But ye have despised the poor.

Do not rich men oppress you, and draw you before the judgment seats? Do not they blaspheme that worthy name by the which ye are called?
