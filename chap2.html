Chapter 2

The Problem With Religious Labels

A label rarely stays between two people. In a congregation, a reputation can travel ahead of someone and quietly influence who trusts them, who invites them to sit nearby, and whose questions receive patience. A description becomes an assumption. The assumption begins shaping ordinary behavior. Before long, people may believe they know someone they have barely spoken to.

Imagine a new member arriving at a church after hearing that a particular person is “trouble.” The warning may have come from someone respected. No one says anything openly when the visitor walks in, yet conversations become guarded and invitations never come. The person may not know what has been said. They only notice the room feels different. A community can call such behavior caution without ever checking whether the story was accurate, complete, or fair.

Religious labels can carry unusual weight because they are often presented as more than personal opinion. A person may be called rebellious, worldly, unclean, compromised, or spiritually dangerous. The label can suggest that the speaker has already settled a question about someone's character and standing before God. Once that impression is established, even ordinary behavior may be interpreted through suspicion.

In Luke 5, Jesus calls Levi, a tax collector, to follow Him. Levi responds by preparing a feast and inviting a large company of people. The religious observers question Jesus' choice of company. Their concern is not simply who is attending dinner. They are troubled by the kind of people with whom Jesus is willing to associate.

“Why do ye eat and drink with publicans and sinners?”

Luke 5:30

Jesus answers that those who are whole do not need a physician in the same way that those who are sick do. He explains that His purpose is to call sinners to repentance. The setting matters: Jesus is not avoiding people whose reputations make others uncomfortable. He is meeting them where they are and making clear why He came.

The crowd's assumptions did not prevent Levi from responding to Jesus. Nor did the opinions of the religious observers determine whether the gathering could become a place where people encountered the Lord. The meal offered a picture of a boundary that others were determined to preserve and a ministry that did not allow that boundary to control every encounter.

James addresses a different form of labeling within a gathering of believers. He describes people being treated differently because of their clothing and wealth. The well-dressed visitor is offered a preferred seat, while the poor person is made to stand or sit in a humiliating place. James does not describe this as a harmless social preference. He challenges the congregation for making distinctions that contradict the faith they profess.

“Have not the faith of our Lord Jesus Christ... with respect of persons.”

James 2:1

Favoritism can be more subtle than the example James gives. A church may treat someone warmly because their family is influential, their work is respected, or their appearance fits the community's expectations. Another person may receive little attention because their circumstances are unfamiliar or uncomfortable. A congregation may never announce such a distinction, yet people learn quickly who is valued and who is tolerated.

We can see a similar pattern when one part of a person's life becomes the only thing others discuss. LGBTQIA+ people may find that their identity is treated as the complete explanation of who they are. Their work, family, friendships, faith, grief, talents, responsibilities, and ordinary joys become secondary. They may be discussed at length without ever being invited into the conversation where conclusions are made.

Imagine a person who has served quietly in a church for years. They have helped with meals, visited people who were sick, supported youth events, and prayed alongside others. When a personal detail becomes known, the conversations change. People who once spoke freely now seem cautious. Some ask questions only to satisfy curiosity. Others make decisions about the person's place in the community without asking how the person is doing. The individual becomes known for a single issue instead of a life that cannot be reduced to one subject.

Being repeatedly defined by others carries a cost. People may rehearse what to disclose, avoid certain conversations, or feel responsible for correcting every rumor. The burden grows when belonging seems conditional upon silence. Listening does not require everyone to reach the same conclusion. Listening refuses to treat someone's humanity as secondary to the conclusion being discussed.

A congregation's welcome is measured in small distinctions. Who is given time to explain? Who receives compassion after a mistake? Whose privacy is protected? Who is believed? Who is left standing alone? The answers reveal a church's culture more honestly than a statement printed on a wall.

Labels can sometimes describe a concern, but they should never become a substitute for knowing a person. The question that follows is where some of our assumptions came from. Traditions can carry wisdom and meaning. A familiar practice, however, may also be mistaken for a command simply because no one has examined the difference.
