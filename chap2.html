Chapter 2

The Problem With Religious Labels

A label rarely remains a word. Within a community, labels can become shortcuts that influence who is trusted, who is invited into conversation, whose questions are taken seriously, and who is kept at a distance. The process may happen so gradually that people no longer recognize the pattern. A description becomes an assumption, the assumption shapes a response, and the response begins to feel like ordinary church culture.

Religious communities use language to communicate beliefs, responsibilities, and shared values. Such language can help people understand their faith. Trouble begins when a term is allowed to stand in for a person's whole story, or when a category is treated as sufficient reason to decide how someone deserves to be received. The word may be familiar to the congregation, but the person carrying the label is still a human being whose circumstances, questions, and experiences deserve to be understood.

Consider the way a reputation can travel ahead of someone. A person arrives at a gathering, and others already know what they have heard. Conversations may become guarded. Invitations may quietly disappear. People may believe they are being prudent while never asking whether the information they received was complete, fair, or even accurate. The individual is left trying to build relationships with people who have already formed an opinion.

This pattern is not limited to one kind of person or one particular disagreement. Religious labels can be attached to people because of their background, finances, education, family circumstances, past decisions, beliefs, or questions. Once a community begins sorting people into categories, those categories can influence treatment more powerfully than the principles the community claims to uphold.

Luke 5:27–32 shows Jesus entering a setting that unsettled religious observers. Levi was a tax collector, and the gathering in his home included others whose reputations drew criticism. The complaint was not simply about a meal. The question revealed how the people at the table had been categorized and how those categories shaped the critics' expectations of Jesus. His answer brought the purpose of His ministry into view: He came to call sinners to repentance. The presence of people in need did not disqualify the gathering from serving a spiritual purpose.

The scene asks us to consider what our own gatherings communicate. Who is comfortable entering? Who expects to be watched? Who believes they must prove their worth before being treated warmly? A congregation may never announce that someone is unwelcome, yet the message can still be communicated through silence, avoidance, selective invitations, whispered conversations, or a noticeable difference in how people are treated.

Labels can also become self-reinforcing. Once a person is considered difficult, untrustworthy, spiritually immature, or unsuitable, ordinary behavior may be interpreted through that conclusion. A question becomes defiance. A boundary becomes selfishness. A mistake becomes proof of character. Meanwhile, behavior that might challenge the label is overlooked. This is how an assumption can quietly become a social reality even when no one is willing to admit that the original judgment may have been incomplete.

James addressed partiality among believers by describing a gathering where people received different treatment based on outward appearance and perceived status. The issue was not simply manners. Favoring one person while humiliating another contradicted the faith they professed. The passage calls the community to examine whether its treatment of people reflects the love of God or the judgments of human preference.

Our welcome is revealed not only by what we say from the platform, but by the distinctions we make when no one is calling attention to them. Who receives patience? Who is believed? Who is given room to explain? Who is treated as a valued member of the community, and who is expected to remain grateful for whatever access is offered? These questions expose the culture created by our everyday decisions.

LGBTQIA+ people can experience this dynamic when the conversation about their identity becomes the only conversation others are willing to have with them. Their faith, family, work, creativity, friendships, service, grief, and ordinary joys may be overlooked. A person can become the subject of discussion without being included in the discussion, leaving others to determine what their life means while never listening to the person living that life.

That experience can create an exhausting burden. Someone may feel compelled to explain themselves repeatedly, anticipate questions before they are asked, or decide which parts of their life are safe to mention. Such a person is not simply navigating disagreement. They are navigating a community where belonging may feel conditional upon remaining quiet or allowing others to define them. Listening can begin to change that dynamic, not by requiring every person to reach identical conclusions, but by refusing to treat anyone as though their humanity were secondary.

The church should examine the culture created by its language. Does a label help someone receive care, accountability, or understanding? Or does the word become a way to keep distance while convincing ourselves that the distance is spiritually necessary? Are we willing to know people beyond the conclusions we have heard about them? Those questions are more useful than defending a label merely because it has been part of our vocabulary for years.

Jesus' ministry repeatedly drew attention to people who were overlooked or dismissed. His presence at Levi's table challenges the assumption that association with people in need compromises the purpose of ministry. The church does not have to choose between thoughtful faith and meaningful relationships. The challenge is to ensure that our beliefs do not become an excuse for treating people as though conversation, kindness, or fellowship must be withheld.

A community's character is revealed in the way people are treated when they do not fit its expectations. We can keep our convictions and still refuse partiality. We can discuss difficult matters without turning people into symbols. We can listen without pretending that every question is simple. We can recognize that a person deserves dignity before we know whether every part of their story aligns with our understanding.

The problem, then, is larger than the label itself. The label may reveal a culture that has learned to sort people before knowing them. That culture deserves examination, especially when its boundaries are defended as though they came directly from God. The next chapter turns to one source of those boundaries: traditions that have been carried so long that people may no longer distinguish what Scripture teaches from what the community has simply learned to expect.

And after these things he went forth, and saw a publican, named Levi, sitting at the receipt of custom: and he said unto him, Follow me. And he left all, rose up, and followed him. And Levi made him a great feast in his own house: and there was a great company of publicans and of others that sat down with them. But their scribes and Pharisees murmured against his disciples, saying, Why do ye eat and drink with publicans and sinners? And Jesus answering said unto them, They that are whole need not a physician; but they that are sick. I came not to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance.

My brethren, have not the faith of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Lord of glory, with respect of persons. For if there come unto your assembly a man with a gold ring, in goodly apparel, and there come in also a poor man in vile raiment; And ye have respect to him that wear the gay clothing, and say unto him, Sit thou here in a good place; and say to the poor, Stand thou there, or sit here under my footstool: Are ye not then partial in yourselves, and are become judges of evil thoughts? Hearken, my beloved brethren, Hath not God chosen the poor of this world rich in faith, and heirs of the kingdom which he hath promised to them that love him? But ye have despised the poor. Do not rich men oppress you, and draw you before the judgment seats? Do not they blaspheme that worthy name by the which ye are called? If ye fulfil the royal law according to the scripture, Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself, ye do well: But if ye have respect to persons, ye commit sin, and are convinced of the law as transgressors.
