Chapter 2

The Problem With Religious Labels

Religious labels often begin with an attempt to define what someone believes, how they live, or where they belong within a particular community. Over time, however, those labels can become more influential than the person they were intended to describe. Rather than encouraging understanding, they can create distance, shape expectations, and determine how someone is treated before a meaningful conversation ever takes place. Within the church, labels may carry additional weight because they are frequently connected to spiritual standing, perceived righteousness, or assumptions about a person’s relationship with God. When a label becomes the primary way we recognize someone, we risk overlooking the humanity, experiences, and individual circumstances that deserve our attention.

Religious language is not inherently harmful, and Scripture contains descriptions that help believers understand spiritual conditions, responsibilities, and choices. The problem arises when language becomes a substitute for understanding or when people use familiar terms without considering the consequences of applying those terms to others. Calling someone rebellious, worldly, compromised, unclean, or spiritually lost may communicate a conclusion, but the conclusion does not necessarily reveal whether the speaker has understood the person, examined the circumstances, or approached the matter with the humility Scripture requires. Words can be accurate in certain contexts, yet careless application can turn a meaningful biblical concept into an instrument of personal judgment.

The Pharisees frequently encountered this problem during the ministry of Jesus. Their concern for religious observance often influenced the distinctions they made between people considered righteous and those viewed as spiritually questionable. In Luke 5:27–32, Jesus was criticized for eating with tax collectors and sinners, people whose reputations made them unwelcome among those who believed their own religious standing required separation. Jesus responded by explaining that those who are well have no need of a physician, but those who are sick do, and He declared that He had come to call sinners to repentance. His response redirected the conversation from the reputation of the people at the table to the purpose of His own ministry. The presence of people who needed spiritual help was not evidence that Jesus had abandoned His mission; their presence was directly connected to the reason He had come.

And after these things he went forth, and saw a publican, named Levi, sitting at the receipt of custom: and he said unto him, Follow me. And he left all, rose up, and followed him. And Levi made him a great feast in his own house: and there was a great company of publicans and of others that sat down with them. But their scribes and Pharisees murmured against his disciples, saying, Why do ye eat and drink with publicans and sinners? And Jesus answering said unto them, They that are whole need not a physician; but they that are sick. I came not to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance.

Luke 5:27–32

The distinction matters because religious communities can become so focused on maintaining an image of purity that they lose sight of the purpose behind their ministry. A congregation may carefully establish expectations for participation, worship, leadership, and conduct, yet still fail to recognize the spiritual and emotional needs of people who arrive seeking God. When maintaining the appearance of righteousness becomes more important than demonstrating the character of Christ, people can begin to feel that their acceptance depends upon satisfying an invisible standard of approval before anyone is willing to know them. Such an atmosphere may discourage honest conversations, encourage people to hide their struggles, and leave individuals believing that their questions or experiences make them unwelcome.

Labels also influence the way people interpret information about someone they have already judged. Once a person has been placed into a particular category, others may begin noticing only the details that reinforce their existing opinion while overlooking evidence that challenges the conclusion. A person’s kindness may be dismissed as an exception, their faith may be questioned because it does not fit an established expectation, and their sincere efforts to serve may receive less attention than the label already attached to them. This pattern can occur within any community, especially when people believe their assumptions are protecting something sacred. Nevertheless, protecting a belief does not justify refusing to see another person clearly.

The experiences of LGBTQIA+ people within religious communities illustrate how labels can influence relationships and opportunities. Someone may be known primarily through a conversation about sexuality or gender, even when that subject represents only one part of a much larger life. Their faith, family relationships, work, friendships, talents, struggles, and desire for spiritual connection may receive little attention because others have already decided what the label means. Such treatment can create an environment where individuals feel they must continually explain themselves or defend their presence, rather than being allowed to participate in ordinary fellowship and meaningful conversations about faith. The resulting distance may have little to do with the person’s willingness to engage spiritually and much to do with the assumptions others have chosen to maintain.

James 2:1–9 addresses the problem of partiality by challenging believers who treat people differently according to outward appearances or perceived social standing. The passage describes a gathering where one person receives favorable treatment because of their appearance and another is treated with less consideration. James identifies such behavior as inconsistent with the faith of those who claim to follow Jesus Christ. His teaching reminds the church that the way people are received matters, particularly when distinctions are made according to characteristics that influence how others perceive their value. The principle extends beyond financial status or outward appearance because partiality can take many forms whenever someone receives greater dignity, attention, or opportunity simply because they are more comfortable for the community to accept.

My brethren, have not the faith of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Lord of glory, with respect of persons. For if there come unto your assembly a man with a gold ring, in goodly apparel, and there come in also a poor man in vile raiment; And ye have respect to him that wear the gay clothing, and say unto him, Sit thou here in a good place; and say to the poor, Stand thou there, or sit here under my footstool: Are ye not then partial in yourselves, and are become judges of evil thoughts? Hearken, my beloved brethren, Hath not God chosen the poor of this world rich in faith, and heirs of the kingdom which he hath promised to them that love him? But ye have despised the poor. Do not rich men oppress you, and draw you before the judgment seats? Do not they blaspheme that worthy name by the which ye are called? If ye fulfil the royal law according to the scripture, Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself, ye do well: But if ye have respect to persons, ye commit sin, and are convinced of the law as transgressors.

James 2:1–9

The difficulty is that religious labels can sometimes make partiality appear spiritually justified. People may believe they are defending holiness when they are actually responding to discomfort, unfamiliarity, or fear of challenging established expectations. The language of conviction can make such decisions difficult to question, especially when the people affected have limited opportunities to explain their experiences or participate in the conversation. A sincere examination of Scripture should therefore include an examination of the motives behind our treatment of others, because the language we use to defend our decisions does not automatically establish that our decisions reflect the character of Christ.

Jesus consistently demonstrated that people deserved to be approached with dignity even when their circumstances raised difficult questions. His conversations were not limited to those who possessed religious influence, social respectability, or a reputation for making acceptable choices. He engaged people whose lives were complicated, whose experiences were misunderstood, and whose presence challenged the expectations of religious observers. His willingness to speak with people others avoided reveals that spiritual concern should never be confused with the desire to preserve distance from those who make us uncomfortable. The example of Christ invites believers to examine whether their relationships are shaped by genuine concern for others or by a desire to maintain boundaries that have become more important than compassion.

This examination does not require Christians to abandon sincere convictions or pretend that theological disagreements no longer exist. Rather, it requires them to recognize that a disagreement about belief does not erase the responsibility to treat another person with dignity. A conversation can remain honest without becoming humiliating, and a conviction can be expressed without reducing someone’s entire identity to a point of disagreement. When believers approach difficult subjects with humility, they create room for meaningful dialogue rather than allowing labels to determine the outcome before the conversation begins.

Religious labels become especially damaging when they are treated as permanent declarations about someone’s character or relationship with God. People grow, learn, change, and develop their understanding through experiences that others may never fully know. A label assigned during one moment may continue following someone long after the circumstances have changed, leaving little room for the possibility of growth or a deeper understanding. Scripture repeatedly calls believers toward spiritual maturity, yet maturity requires the willingness to recognize that our understanding of other people may remain incomplete. We should be careful about making declarations that leave no room for the work of God in another person’s life.

The church must therefore examine not only the labels it uses but also the culture those labels create. Are people encouraged to ask questions, share experiences, and pursue spiritual growth, or are they expected to remain silent because their circumstances make others uncomfortable? Are individuals welcomed into meaningful relationships, or are they kept at a distance until they satisfy expectations that have never been clearly explained? Does the congregation communicate that every person deserves dignity, or does the way people are treated reveal that dignity is distributed according to approval? These questions help expose whether religious language is serving the purpose of spiritual understanding or reinforcing divisions that prevent people from seeing one another clearly.

A label may describe how someone is perceived, but it cannot fully explain who that person is. No single word captures the complexity of a human life, the depth of someone’s experiences, or the sincerity of their relationship with God. Christians should be cautious about allowing familiar terminology to replace the responsibility of listening, understanding, and responding with compassion. The words we choose can either encourage meaningful connection or create unnecessary distance, and the responsibility for that choice belongs to the person speaking.

The problem with religious labels is not simply that people sometimes use the wrong words. The deeper problem occurs when the words become more important than the people, when assumptions become more influential than understanding, and when the desire to define someone takes precedence over the responsibility to love them. A church that genuinely seeks to represent Christ must remain willing to examine the language it uses and the attitudes that language reveals.

Before assigning another label, we should consider whether the word brings understanding or merely reinforces a judgment we have already made. We should ask whether our language reflects careful examination of Scripture, whether our motives are rooted in compassion, and whether our conduct demonstrates the dignity Jesus extended to those He encountered. The responsibility to speak truth remains important, yet the responsibility to treat people as human beings created in the image of God must never disappear beneath the weight of religious terminology.

When labels become barriers to understanding, the church must be willing to remove those barriers and begin seeing people again.
