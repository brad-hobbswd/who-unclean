Chapter 8

Jesus and the People Others Rejected

The Gospels give us scenes worth slowing down for. Rather than beginning with a lesson about who was rejected, watch what happens when Jesus meets people and when others react to His choices. In these encounters, the story itself reveals the difference between the way people are categorized and the way Jesus sees them.

Zacchaeus was known in Jericho. His occupation and wealth shaped the reputation he carried, and the crowd did not need much explanation when Jesus chose to visit his home. They murmured. Jesus, however, called him by name and invited him down from the tree. The encounter was personal before the crowd's opinion could become the defining moment.

And Jesus entered and passed through Jericho. And, behold, there was a man named Zacchaeus, which was the chief among the publicans, and he was rich. And he sought to see Jesus who he was; and could not for the press, because he was little of stature. And he ran before, and climbed up into a sycomore tree to see him: for he was to pass that way. And when Jesus came to the place, he looked up, and saw him, and said unto him, Zacchaeus, make haste, and come down; for to day I must abide at thy house. And he made haste, and came down, and received him joyfully. And when they saw it, they all murmured, saying, That he was gone to be guest with a man that is a sinner. And Zacchaeus stood, and said unto the Lord; Behold, Lord, the half of my goods I give to the poor; and if I have taken any thing from any man by false accusation, I restore him fourfold. And Jesus said unto him, This day is salvation come to this house, forsomuch as he also is a son of Abraham. For the Son of man is come to seek and to save that which was lost.

Luke 19:1–10

Zacchaeus received Jesus joyfully. The encounter led him to speak about restitution and generosity. Jesus declared salvation had come to the house. The story does not ask us to pretend Zacchaeus's conduct had no consequences. His response shows a changed direction. What matters in the scene is that Jesus did not allow the crowd's verdict to prevent the encounter that brought change.

Notice the sequence. Jesus saw him. Jesus spoke to him. Jesus entered his home. The crowd complained. Zacchaeus responded. The story moves through an encounter rather than through a public shaming. Jesus did not need the crowd's permission to reach someone they had already dismissed.

A man with leprosy came to Jesus with a question that carries its own pain: “If thou wilt, thou canst make me clean.” He believed Jesus had power. What he was uncertain about was willingness. Jesus was moved with compassion, reached out, touched him, and answered directly. The touch comes before the man's cleansing is complete in the narrative. Jesus does not recoil from the person who has approached Him.

And there came a leper to him, beseeching him, and kneeling down to him, and saying unto him, If thou wilt, thou canst make me clean. And Jesus, moved with compassion, put forth his hand, and touched him, and saith unto him, I will; be thou clean. And as soon as he had spoken, immediately the leprosy departed from him, and he was cleansed. And he straitly charged him, and forthwith sent him away; And saith unto him, See thou say nothing to any man: but go thy way, shew thyself to the priest, and offer for thy cleansing those things which Moses commanded, for a testimony unto them. But he went out, and began to publish it much, and to blaze abroad the matter, insomuch that Jesus could no more openly enter into the city, but was without in desert places: and they came to him from every quarter.

Mark 1:40–45

The man's question is worth hearing without rushing past it. He did not ask whether Jesus was capable. He asked whether Jesus was willing. There are people who approach church with a similar uncertainty, not necessarily about God's power, but about whether anyone will make room for them, listen to them, or respond with compassion. Jesus' action in this scene gives us something concrete to consider: His compassion was not merely expressed from a distance.

Then there are the children. People brought them to Jesus, and the disciples rebuked those bringing them. The disciples appeared to believe the children were not important enough to interrupt. Jesus was displeased. He welcomed them, took them into His arms, and blessed them. The scene is brief, but the response is unmistakable.

And they brought young children to him, that he should touch them: and his disciples rebuked those that brought them. But when Jesus saw it, he was much displeased, and said unto them, Suffer the little children to come unto me, and forbid them not: for of such is the kingdom of God. Verily I say unto you, Whosoever shall not receive the kingdom of God as a little child, he shall not enter therein. And he took them up in his arms, put his hands upon them, and blessed them.

Mark 10:13–16

The disciples had made a decision about who deserved access to Jesus. Jesus corrected them. The children were not an inconvenience to be managed. Their presence mattered. The passage reminds us that even those who believe they are helping can become barriers when they decide too quickly who should be allowed near.

These stories are different. Zacchaeus was a tax collector. The man with leprosy lived with a condition that affected his place in community. The children were being dismissed as unimportant. The passages should not be collapsed into one identical circumstance. Together, however, they show Jesus responding to individuals rather than allowing the opinions or assumptions of others to determine His actions.

The church does not need to manufacture a comparison for every person who has experienced rejection. The Gospel accounts already challenge us to notice the way Jesus responds. He sees individuals. He approaches. He listens and speaks. He demonstrates compassion. He corrects those who prevent others from coming near. His ministry is not characterized by indifference toward people who have been pushed aside.

For LGBTQIA+ people who have been discussed, excluded, or made to feel like a disruption, these stories may invite a simple but meaningful question: when someone comes near, are we behaving like those who obstruct, or are we learning from Jesus? The question is about our conduct. It asks whether we notice the person, whether we make room for conversation, and whether we allow someone to be treated with dignity rather than becoming a subject of public scrutiny.

Jesus' compassion was not a performance designed to impress the crowd. His actions were rooted in the purpose of His ministry. He spoke with authority, called people toward God, and responded to human need. We should resist separating His truth from His treatment of people. Both are visible in the Gospel accounts.

The examples leave us with something practical. We can notice who is standing alone. We can stop a conversation that has turned someone into gossip. We can welcome a visitor without interrogating them. We can correct our own behavior when someone tells us we have caused harm. We can make sure that the people who come seeking prayer or fellowship are not treated as interruptions.

The question is not whether every situation will be simple. The question is whether our response resembles the Jesus we claim to follow. The people others overlooked were not invisible to Him. Their presence did not threaten His authority. Their circumstances did not prevent Him from approaching them. That example deserves more than admiration; it calls us to practice the same attentiveness in our own communities.
