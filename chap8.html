Chapter 8

Jesus and the People Others Rejected

The Gospel accounts invite us to watch what happens when Jesus meets people and when others react to His choices. The encounters reveal the difference between a crowd's assumptions and the attention Jesus gives to the person standing before Him. Rather than rushing toward a lesson, consider the details of each meeting.

Zacchaeus was known in Jericho for his occupation and wealth. He wanted to see Jesus, but the crowd made that difficult. He climbed a tree, hoping to catch a glimpse as Jesus passed. The scene could have ended with the crowd continuing along its way, yet Jesus stopped, called him by name, and invited him to come down.

“Zacchaeus, make haste, and come down; for to day I must abide at thy house.”

Luke 19:5

The crowd murmured when Jesus chose to visit his home. Zacchaeus, however, received Him joyfully. The encounter led him to speak about restitution and generosity. The story does not erase the consequences of his conduct. The crowd's judgment did not prevent Jesus from meeting him, and that meeting became a turning point. Notice the sequence: Jesus saw him, spoke to him, entered his home, and the encounter brought a response. Public shaming was not the method Jesus chose.

A man with leprosy approached Jesus with a question that carries its own ache. He believed Jesus had power to make him clean. What he did not know was whether Jesus was willing.

“If thou wilt, thou canst make me clean.”

Mark 1:40

Jesus was moved with compassion. He reached out, touched the man, and answered directly that He was willing. The touch comes before the man's cleansing is complete in the narrative. Jesus does not recoil from the person who has approached Him. The compassion is not offered from a safe distance.

The man's question is worth hearing without rushing past it. He did not ask whether Jesus was capable. He asked whether Jesus was willing. There are people who approach church with a similar uncertainty, not necessarily about God's power, but about whether anyone will make room for them, listen to them, or respond with compassion. The Gospel scene gives us something concrete to consider: compassion was expressed through action.

Then there are the children. People brought them to Jesus, but the disciples rebuked those bringing them. The disciples appeared to believe the children were not important enough to interrupt. Jesus was displeased and corrected the barrier they had created.

“Suffer the little children to come unto me, and forbid them not.”

Mark 10:14

Jesus welcomed them, took them into His arms, and blessed them. The scene is brief, but the response is unmistakable. The disciples had made a decision about who deserved access to Jesus. The children were not an inconvenience to be managed. Their presence mattered.

These encounters are different. Zacchaeus was a tax collector. The man with leprosy lived with a condition that affected his place in community. The children were being dismissed as unimportant. We should not collapse these circumstances into one identical experience. Together, however, they show Jesus responding to individuals rather than allowing other people's opinions to determine His actions.

The church does not need to manufacture a comparison for every person who has experienced rejection. The Gospel accounts already challenge us to notice how Jesus responds. He sees individuals. He approaches. He speaks. He demonstrates compassion. He corrects those who prevent others from coming near. His ministry is not characterized by indifference toward people who have been pushed aside.

For LGBTQIA+ people who have been discussed, excluded, or made to feel like a disruption, these stories may invite a practical question: when someone comes near, are we behaving like those who obstruct, or are we learning from Jesus? The question is about our conduct. Do we notice the person? Do we make room for conversation? Do we allow someone to be treated with dignity rather than becoming a subject of public scrutiny?

Jesus' compassion was not a performance designed to impress the crowd. His actions were rooted in the purpose of His ministry. He spoke with authority, called people toward God, and responded to human need. We should resist separating His truth from His treatment of people. Both are visible in the Gospel accounts.

We can notice who is standing alone. We can stop a conversation that has turned someone into gossip. We can welcome a visitor without interrogating them. We can correct our own behavior when someone tells us we have caused harm. We can make sure people who come seeking prayer or fellowship are not treated as interruptions. These are not grand gestures. They are ordinary ways of practicing what we claim to believe.

The question is not whether every situation will be simple. The question is whether our response resembles the Jesus we claim to follow. The people others overlooked were not invisible to Him. Their presence did not threaten His authority. Their circumstances did not prevent Him from approaching them. That example deserves more than admiration; it calls us to practice the same attentiveness in our own communities.

The next chapter turns to the moments when people do not agree. Compassion must continue beyond the first welcome, especially when a conversation becomes difficult and convictions are tested.
