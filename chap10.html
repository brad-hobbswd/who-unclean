Chapter 10

Stop Calling Unclean What God Has Made Holy

The question in the title of this book reaches its fullest weight when we stop directing the question toward someone else and allow the question to examine us. Who are you to call unclean what God has made holy? The question asks us to consider the authority behind our words, the certainty with which we have spoken, and the people who have been affected by judgments we may never have reconsidered.

Throughout these pages, we have moved from the pain of being labeled to the culture those labels can create. We have examined traditions that may become walls, followed Peter as his understanding was challenged, considered the responsibility of spiritual leadership, listened to the experiences of LGBTQIA+ people who have been pushed away, and watched Jesus encounter people others overlooked. The purpose was not to repeat every argument at the end. The purpose was to bring us to a responsibility we cannot leave on the page.

Acts 10 remains an important part of that responsibility. Peter's encounter with Cornelius concerns God's inclusion of Gentiles and the recognition that Peter must not regard people as common or unclean. The passage must be read in its context. Peter's words nevertheless challenge us to examine the human judgments we have attached to people and whether those judgments have been mistaken for God's own declaration.

The church is still responsible for studying Scripture carefully. Honest interpretation matters. Convictions matter. Accountability matters. None of those responsibilities gives us permission to forget the person standing in front of us. We can acknowledge difficult questions without turning a human being into the question itself. We can speak with sincerity while remaining humble enough to recognize the limits of our understanding.

Perhaps someone reading this book has spent years wondering whether they can enter a church without being scrutinized. Perhaps they have been told that their presence is a problem, that their prayers are unwelcome, or that they must hide parts of their life to remain in a community they love. They may not need another argument today. They may need someone to listen, to treat them with dignity, and to stop making them carry the burden of proving that they deserve kindness.

Perhaps another reader has spoken words they now regret. They may have believed they were protecting truth and never considered the effect their words had upon someone else. The invitation is not to remain trapped in shame. The invitation is to take responsibility, listen honestly, apologize when necessary, and let changed conduct demonstrate what repentance means. We do not have to defend every sentence we once spoke in order to remain faithful to God.

Belonging is not built through a slogan. It grows through ordinary decisions: whether we welcome someone warmly, protect their privacy, refuse gossip, make room for questions, notice who has been left alone, and remain present when a conversation becomes uncomfortable. A congregation's culture is shaped by those choices long before a visitor reads its statement of belief.

No one should have to become the subject of a church's debate to be treated as a human being. No one should have to disclose private details to earn basic respect. No one should be reduced to a label while the rest of their life remains unseen. The people we encounter are more than the assumptions we carry about them, and our responsibility is to approach them with care.

The work does not end with understanding. What we have considered should influence the next conversation, the next invitation, the next decision, and the next time we hear someone being spoken about in a way that strips away their dignity. We can choose to interrupt gossip. We can ask better questions. We can acknowledge harm. We can refuse to use spiritual language as a cover for contempt.

We may not have every answer. We can remain students of Scripture, students of one another's experiences, and people willing to let God correct us. A teachable heart is not a weak heart. Humility does not make truth less important. Humility helps us remember that God is God, and we are not.

So let the question remain with us, not as a weapon to throw at someone else, but as an invitation to examine our own lives. Before we pronounce judgment, before we close a door, before we decide that someone is beyond our welcome, let us ask whether our words and actions reflect the mercy we ourselves have received.

God alone knows the heart completely. May we follow Him with sincerity, care for our neighbors, and remain teachable when His direction leads beyond familiar boundaries. May the people who encounter us find honesty without cruelty, conviction without contempt, and compassion that is visible in the way we live.

When these pages are closed, the next chapter is written through our conduct. Who will we welcome? Whom will we listen to? What will we reconsider? Where will we make room for someone who has been standing at a distance?

Let our lives show that we have heard the question. Let our treatment of others reflect the mercy we have received.

And he said unto them, Ye know how that it is an unlawful thing for a man that is a Jew to keep company, or come unto one of another nation; but God hath shewed me that I should not call any man common or unclean.
