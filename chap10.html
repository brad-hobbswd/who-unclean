Chapter 10

Stop Calling Unclean What God Has Made Holy

The question that began this journey was never meant to remain a question directed toward someone else. “Who are you to call unclean what God has made holy?” challenges every believer who has become comfortable making judgments about people without examining the authority behind those judgments. The question reaches beyond a particular congregation, denomination, interpretation, or generation. It calls the church to examine the words spoken, the traditions defended, and the people who have been left standing outside.

Peter’s experience in Acts 10 reminds us that spiritual growth sometimes requires us to reconsider what we believed we understood. Peter was not a stranger to Scripture. He was a disciple who had walked with Jesus, witnessed miracles, and heard the teachings of the Lord. Nevertheless, God revealed something Peter had not fully understood. The revelation challenged his assumptions and required him to respond through obedience.

Peter’s journey did not end with the vision. The revelation became meaningful through his willingness to enter Cornelius’s home, listen to the people gathered there, and witness the work of God. His understanding expanded through an encounter that could not be dismissed as merely theoretical. The people before him were no longer distant subjects of religious discussion. They were human beings whom God was reaching.

The church must be willing to recognize that our understanding can grow through encounters with people whose lives differ from our own. A conversation may reveal pain we never knew existed. A testimony may challenge assumptions that were formed without personal experience. Someone’s sincere questions may expose areas where our understanding has become more dependent upon tradition than careful examination of Scripture.

Such moments require humility. We must resist the temptation to defend every previous statement simply because we once spoke confidently. The willingness to examine our words is not weakness. The willingness to acknowledge that someone has been hurt by our actions is not an abandonment of faith. A sincere desire to follow God should make us willing to correct ourselves when our conduct fails to reflect His character.

Throughout this book, we have examined the power of labels, the danger of religious traditions becoming barriers, the revelation given to Peter, the responsibility of spiritual leadership, and the experiences of LGBTQIA+ individuals who have encountered rejection within religious communities. We have also considered the example of Jesus, who consistently recognized the dignity of people others overlooked.

These discussions should lead beyond words. A church that recognizes the harm caused by rejection must consider how relationships can be rebuilt. A believer who realizes that an assumption has caused unnecessary pain must be willing to listen and respond with sincerity. A congregation that desires to represent Christ must consider whether its welcome is reflected through actual relationships rather than statements made from a platform.

The people who have been pushed away should not be expected to carry the entire responsibility for restoring relationships. Those who have spoken, acted, or created environments that caused harm must also be willing to examine their part. Apologies matter. Changed behavior matters. Listening without defensiveness matters. A promise of love becomes meaningful when the person receiving that promise can recognize love through consistent actions.

The central question of this book does not require us to stop studying Scripture. The question calls us to study with humility, recognizing the seriousness of speaking in God’s name. We must be careful not to confuse our own assumptions with the voice of the Lord. We must also recognize the difference between biblical authority and the authority we sometimes assign to our personal interpretations.

Acts 10:15 declares, “What God hath cleansed, that call not thou common.” Peter’s experience demonstrates that God can challenge the boundaries His people have established through incomplete understanding. The passage directly concerns God’s inclusion of Gentiles and Peter’s realization that he should not regard people as common or unclean. That context should remain central to our reading. The application also challenges the church to examine the ways human judgments have been placed upon people whom God is reaching.

The question of LGBTQIA+ inclusion involves broader biblical interpretation, theological convictions, and conversations that Christians continue to approach differently. Those discussions deserve honesty rather than oversimplification. Yet disagreement should never become permission to dehumanize people, deny their dignity, spread harmful assumptions, or treat them as though the presence of someone different threatens the holiness of God.

We must never allow our desire to defend a position to become greater than our desire to reflect Christ. The church is not strengthened through humiliation. The gospel is not advanced through cruelty. People are not drawn toward God through unnecessary rejection. Our words, decisions, relationships, and attitudes should reflect the faith we profess.

Perhaps someone reading these pages has spent years wondering whether there is room for them in a church. Perhaps someone else has spent years believing they were protecting the faith while never considering how their actions affected people standing nearby. Both deserve an opportunity to encounter truth with humility. Both deserve conversations that recognize their humanity. Both need the reminder that God remains greater than our understanding.

The church must remain willing to hear the voice of God, even when the revelation challenges familiar beliefs. We must be willing to examine our traditions, our language, our leadership, and our treatment of people. We must never become so confident in our own position that we refuse to recognize the possibility of having misunderstood someone, misrepresented Scripture, or placed a burden upon another person that God never instructed us to place.

Peter entered Cornelius’s home because God had challenged his understanding. The church today must remain willing to follow God’s leading rather than allowing fear, pride, or unfamiliarity to determine whom we approach. The people God is reaching may not look like the people we expected. Their stories may challenge us. Their questions may require patience. Their presence may call us toward deeper understanding.

The final responsibility belongs to every believer who has ever spoken a judgment over another human being. Before declaring someone unclean, before deciding who belongs outside the fellowship, before turning an interpretation into a verdict against another person’s life, we must ask whether we have truly heard God.

Who are you to call unclean what God has made holy?

May the church never forget that God is the One who cleanses, God is the One who calls, God is the One who saves, and God is the One who knows the heart. Our responsibility is to follow Him faithfully, love people sincerely, examine ourselves honestly, and remain humble enough to hear His voice when He calls us beyond the boundaries we have built.

The question remains. The responsibility remains. The invitation to examine our hearts remains.

Stop calling unclean what God has made holy.
