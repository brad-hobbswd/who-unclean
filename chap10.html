Chapter 10

Stop Calling Unclean What God Has Made Holy

The question in the title reaches its weight when we stop directing the question at someone else and allow the question to examine us. Who are you to call unclean what God has made holy? What authority lies behind our words, and how certain are we that our judgment reflects God's own declaration?

Peter's words in Acts 10 arose from a specific encounter. He had been taught to observe boundaries, received a vision that challenged his understanding, and then entered Cornelius's home. There, he recognized that God had been working among people he had not expected to receive in the same way. The declaration was not made from a distance. Peter's understanding was shaped by the people he met and the evidence of God's work among them.

“God hath shewed me that I should not call any man common or unclean.”

Acts 10:28

Acts 10 describes God's inclusion of Gentiles and Peter's recognition that he must not regard people as common or unclean. The context matters. The passage does not answer every theological question by itself. Yet Peter's words should make us cautious about attaching our own judgments to God's name without careful examination.

Throughout these chapters, we have considered the pain of being labeled, the way labels influence community, the traditions we inherit, Peter's revelation, the responsibility of spiritual authority, the experiences of people pushed away, the encounters Jesus had with those others dismissed, and the way we conduct ourselves when convictions differ. Each subject brings us back to a choice about how we treat people.

Study Scripture seriously. Hold convictions honestly. Remain accountable for the way those convictions are expressed. None of these responsibilities requires reducing a person to the question being debated. A human being standing before us deserves to be heard, not turned into an argument or an example used to prove our point.

Someone reading these pages may have spent years wondering whether church is safe, whether prayer is welcome, or whether belonging requires hiding. They may need a patient listener more than another speech. Someone else may recognize words they once spoke and regret the harm caused. Responsibility begins with listening, acknowledging the hurt, apologizing without excuses, and allowing changed behavior to speak.

Belonging grows through ordinary choices. We welcome someone. We protect their privacy. We refuse gossip. We make room for questions. We notice who has been left alone. We remain present when a conversation becomes uncomfortable. No one should have to disclose private details or become the subject of a debate to receive basic respect.

We may not have every answer. We can remain students of Scripture and of one another's experiences, willing for God to correct us. Humility does not make truth less important; humility reminds us that God is God and we are not. The ability to say “I need to study this further” or “I may have misunderstood” is not weakness. It is a willingness to remain teachable.

Perhaps the most important work begins after reading. Before closing a door or pronouncing judgment, ask whether our words reflect the mercy we have received. Before repeating a story about someone, consider whether the story is ours to tell. Before demanding that another person explain their life, consider whether we have offered the kind of listening that makes an honest conversation possible.

When the book is closed, the next chapter is written in the welcome we offer, the people we listen to, and the harm we are willing to repair. May those who encounter us find honesty without cruelty, conviction without contempt, and compassion visible in the way we live.
