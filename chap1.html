Chapter 1

Who Gave You That Label?

Labels have a way of following people long after the person who assigned the label has forgotten the conversation. A single comment, an assumption about someone’s character, or a judgment made without understanding the circumstances can shape how others perceive a person for years. Religious labels carry an especially heavy weight because they are often spoken with the authority of Scripture, leaving the recipient to wonder whether the rejection came from people or from God Himself. When someone is called unclean, unworthy, rebellious, unacceptable, or beyond redemption, the words can reach places that ordinary criticism never touches. The person hearing those words may begin questioning their relationship with God, their value as a human being, and whether a place exists for them among people who claim to represent Jesus.

The church has a responsibility to recognize the weight carried by words spoken in the name of God. Christians are called to share truth, encourage spiritual growth, and demonstrate the character of Christ, yet those responsibilities do not grant permission to make careless declarations about another person’s worth. A label may seem harmless to the person speaking, especially when the language has become familiar within a particular religious community, while the consequences for the person receiving the label may be deeply personal. Words that are repeated from one generation to another can eventually become accepted as unquestionable, even when nobody stops to consider whether the language reflects Scripture accurately or the compassion Jesus demonstrated.

Throughout the Bible, God encounters people whose lives do not fit comfortably within the expectations of those around them. Religious leaders frequently made distinctions between people they considered acceptable and people they believed should remain outside their circles. Their confidence in those distinctions sometimes prevented them from recognizing the work of God happening directly in front of them. Jesus repeatedly challenged the assumption that religious reputation, outward appearance, social position, or familiarity with religious customs automatically revealed the condition of a person’s heart. His interactions with people often exposed the distance between what religious observers believed about holiness and what God was actually demonstrating through mercy.

The story of the Samaritan woman in John 4:7–26 offers a meaningful example. Jesus approached a woman whose background, community, and personal circumstances created several reasons for others to avoid meaningful conversation with her. The encounter did not begin with a public declaration about her worth or a demand that she first prove herself acceptable. Jesus engaged her in conversation, revealed truth about Himself, and allowed the exchange to develop into a moment of spiritual significance. The woman eventually returned to her community carrying news about the man she had encountered, and many people came to believe through her testimony. Her story reminds us that a person’s circumstances do not prevent God from reaching them, and someone whom others may overlook can become a witness to a profound encounter with Christ.

7 There cometh a woman of Samaria to draw water: Jesus saith unto her, Give me to drink.

8 (For his disciples were gone away unto the city to buy meat.)

9 Then saith the woman of Samaria unto him, How is it that thou, being a Jew, askest drink of me, which am a woman of Samaria? for the Jews have no dealings with the Samaritans.

John 4:7–26

The danger of labeling people becomes especially serious when personal assumptions are mistaken for divine authority. A person may sincerely believe that a particular judgment reflects God’s position, yet sincerity alone does not guarantee accuracy. Religious conviction must remain accountable to Scripture, context, humility, and the responsibility to love others. Christians should be willing to examine the words they use, the conclusions they reach, and the influence those conclusions have upon the people listening. A declaration spoken confidently from a pulpit or repeated within a congregation can shape someone’s understanding of God for a long time, making careful interpretation and compassionate communication essential responsibilities rather than optional qualities.

Members of the LGBTQIA+ community have often encountered labels that reduce their lives to a single subject, leaving little room for their faith, character, relationships, gifts, experiences, or individual stories. Some have been treated as though their presence creates a spiritual problem for the congregation, while others have been made to feel that their participation must remain limited regardless of their sincere desire to worship, serve, and grow in faith. Such experiences deserve thoughtful consideration, especially when the people involved are seeking a relationship with God rather than attempting to provoke conflict. A church may disagree about theological questions, yet disagreement should never become an excuse for humiliation, cruelty, or the denial of another person’s dignity.

The question raised by this chapter is not an invitation to disregard biblical teaching or assume that every disagreement can be resolved through personal preference. Rather, the question challenges the authority behind our declarations. Where does Scripture speak clearly, where must interpretation be approached carefully, and where have our personal assumptions become so familiar that we no longer recognize the difference between our own conclusions and the words of God? Honest examination requires more than repeating what we have always heard. Such examination requires a willingness to read Scripture in context, listen to people whose experiences differ from our own, and acknowledge that our understanding may need to grow.

Jesus warned His followers about the danger of judging others while neglecting their own need for examination. In Matthew 7:1–5, He described the person who notices a speck in another person’s eye while overlooking a beam in their own. The illustration does not dismiss the importance of helping someone or recognizing wrongdoing; rather, the teaching confronts the hypocrisy of approaching another person’s condition without first examining ourselves. A heart committed to correction should also be committed to humility, recognizing that spiritual growth involves continual examination of our motives, attitudes, and conduct. The responsibility to speak truth should never become a convenient way to avoid addressing the condition of our own hearts.

1 Judge not, that ye be not judged.

2 For with what judgment ye judge, ye shall be judged: and with what measure ye mete, it shall be measured to you again.

3 And why beholdest thou the mote that is in thy brother’s eye, but considerest not the beam that is in thine own eye?

4 Or how wilt thou say to thy brother, Let me pull out the mote out of thine eye; and, behold, a beam is in thine own eye?

5 Thou hypocrite, first cast out the beam out of thine own eye; and then shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote out of thy brother’s eye.

Matthew 7:1–5

Before assigning another person a label, we should consider what our words reveal about us. Are we speaking from a genuine desire to encourage spiritual growth, or are we protecting ourselves from people whose lives challenge our assumptions? Are we approaching a conversation with humility, or have we already decided that our interpretation cannot be questioned? Do our words reflect the patience and compassion of Christ, or have we become comfortable speaking about people without considering the pain our declarations may cause? These questions deserve honest answers, especially from those who carry responsibility within the church.

A label cannot tell the whole story of a human life, and our limited understanding should remind us to approach others with care. People are more than the circumstances surrounding their lives, more than the mistakes they have made, more than the opinions others hold about them, and more than the categories into which society or religion places them. Every person carries experiences that may never be fully understood by those observing from a distance. Recognizing that reality should encourage us to listen before speaking, seek understanding before reaching conclusions, and remember that dignity is not something we grant another person according to our approval.

The church does not lose the authority of Scripture by treating people with compassion, nor does humility weaken the responsibility to pursue truth. Compassion allows truth to be communicated without unnecessary harm, while humility reminds us that our understanding remains accountable to God. The challenge is to resist the temptation to make ourselves the final authority over another person’s relationship with Him. We can examine Scripture, discuss difficult questions, and pursue sincere convictions while recognizing the seriousness of speaking on behalf of God.

The title of this chapter asks a question that should remain with us beyond the final paragraph. Who gave you that label, and what authority did that person possess when the declaration was made? Was the label rooted in careful biblical understanding, or was the judgment shaped by tradition, fear, personal discomfort, or assumptions that had never been examined? The answers may require us to reconsider familiar language and acknowledge that some declarations have caused harm without accomplishing the spiritual purpose their speakers intended.

Before calling another person unclean, we must be willing to examine the authority behind our words, the condition of our hearts, and the responsibility we carry when representing Jesus Christ. A faith that continually examines itself will remain open to correction, while a faith that assumes every judgment must be right may overlook the very people God is calling us to love.

The question is not merely whether we have labeled someone. The deeper question is whether we have mistaken our own judgment for the voice of God.
