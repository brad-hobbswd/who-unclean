Chapter 1

Who Gave You That Label?

Some words stay with us long after the conversation has ended. A person may forget the exact room, the faces around them, or what happened immediately afterward, yet remember the sentence that changed how they saw themselves. When a label is spoken by someone believed to represent God, the words can carry a weight far beyond the speaker’s intention. The person receiving them may begin asking questions that were never theirs to carry: Am I welcome? Has God rejected me? Is there something about me that makes love, fellowship, or prayer inaccessible?

Perhaps the label was spoken from a pulpit. Perhaps it came during a private conversation, a family gathering, a prayer line, or a moment when someone was already struggling to find the courage to be honest. Sometimes a word is delivered as though no further conversation is necessary. The speaker moves on, while the person who heard the declaration is left trying to reconcile the words with a faith they may have treasured for years.

Religious language can reach deeply into a person's identity. Words such as unclean, rebellious, unacceptable, or lost may sound like descriptions to the person speaking, but the listener can hear a verdict. The difference matters. A description invites understanding; a verdict can make someone feel that their story has already been decided and that no one is interested in hearing the rest.

Many people know what that experience feels like. They have been reduced to one part of their lives, one question, one mistake, one difference, or one assumption. Their friendships, generosity, faith, talents, responsibilities, and hopes disappear behind the word someone chose to use. They may still attend church, sing, pray, serve, and believe, yet carry the quiet concern that their presence is tolerated rather than welcomed.

This is not an abstract concern. A person who has been spoken about rather than spoken with may begin avoiding the very conversations that could help them grow. Someone who has been embarrassed publicly may find private prayer difficult afterward. A person who has been told that God cannot accept them may struggle to separate the voice of a religious community from the voice of God. Such wounds deserve more than a quick reminder to move on. They deserve honesty, care, and a willingness to understand what our words have done.

The question is not whether every difficult word can be avoided. Faith sometimes requires honest conversations, correction, and careful examination. The question is whether we have treated the person before us as someone worthy of listening, or whether we have used a label to end the conversation before we ever understood their circumstances. A conclusion reached without listening can carry the appearance of certainty while missing the very person we claim to be addressing.

Jesus warned His followers about the danger of examining another person while ignoring their own need for examination. His illustration of the speck and the beam is not a permission to overlook every concern. The warning is about the posture from which we approach someone else. A person who intends to help must first be willing to see clearly, and clear sight begins with humility about our own condition.

Before correction becomes our first response, self-examination should be our first responsibility. What has shaped the way we see this person? Are we responding to what we actually know, or to a conclusion formed long ago? Have we listened carefully? Are we willing to discover that our words were careless, that our assumptions were incomplete, or that the person in front of us has been carrying pain we never noticed?

The people who have experienced religious labeling are not all asking for the same thing. Some want an apology. Some want a conversation without being interrupted. Some need time and distance. Others are trying to understand whether a relationship with God can survive the words spoken by people who claimed to speak for Him. Their experiences cannot be summarized by one response, and we should not presume to know what healing must look like for every person.

The responsibility of the church is not made lighter by the fact that words can be spoken unintentionally. Intent matters, yet impact deserves attention too. A person may have meant to defend a belief and still have caused unnecessary harm. A willingness to hear that truth does not automatically mean abandoning conviction. It means recognizing that our conduct, our tone, and our care for another person are also part of the witness we offer.

Matthew 7:1–5 calls us to examine ourselves before making ourselves responsible for another person's correction. The passage asks us to take our own condition seriously. That is a demanding instruction, especially when we feel certain that someone else's life needs attention. The certainty we feel should not excuse the examination we are commanded to undertake.

The question in this chapter is personal: who gave you that label, and what did you believe the words meant when you heard them? Perhaps the declaration came from someone whose authority you trusted. Perhaps you have repeated similar language to someone else. Either way, the question invites us to look honestly at the weight of words and the responsibility that comes with speaking them.

A human being is never fully known through a label. Our understanding is limited, our perspective can be incomplete, and our words can reach further than we intended. That recognition should make us slower to pronounce judgment and more willing to listen. The next step is to look beyond the individual words and consider how religious labels begin shaping the way communities see, treat, and make room for people.

Judge not, that ye be not judged. For with what judgment ye judge, ye shall be judged: and with what measure ye mete, it shall be measured to you again.

And why beholdest thou the mote that is in thy brother’s eye, but considerest not the beam that is in thine own eye? Or how wilt thou say to thy brother, Let me pull out the mote out of thine eye; and, behold, a beam is in thine own eye?

Thou hypocrite, first cast out the beam out of thine own eye; and then shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote out of thy brother’s eye.

Matthew 7:1–5
