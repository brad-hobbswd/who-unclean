Chapter 1

Who Gave You That Label?

Some words stay with us long after the room has emptied. We may forget who was standing nearby or what happened next, yet remember the sentence that changed how we saw ourselves. A label spoken by someone believed to represent God can sound less like an opinion and more like a verdict. The words may leave us asking questions we never thought we would have to ask: Am I welcome here? Has God rejected me? Have I misunderstood the faith I once held so close?

Sometimes the label comes from a pulpit. Sometimes from a parent, a friend, a teacher, or a private conversation after church. The speaker may believe the words were necessary. The person who heard them may carry the weight long after the speaker has moved on. One sentence can change how someone walks into a sanctuary, how freely they pray, or whether they feel comfortable mentioning the people they love.

A label has a way of shrinking a person. A whole life becomes reduced to one description. The kindness someone has shown, the responsibilities they carry, the work they do, the prayers they have prayed, the grief they have survived, and the love they give to others can all disappear behind a word. Once that happens, people may begin responding to the label rather than the human being standing in front of them.

Think about someone who has attended church for years. They know the songs, have helped prepare events, prayed with others, and have found genuine comfort in worship. One day, a conversation turns toward a part of their life they have never felt safe discussing. The response is immediate and severe. Rather than being asked questions, they are told what their life means. Rather than being heard, they become an example in someone else's lesson.

Afterward, the person may still believe in God. They may still read Scripture and pray. The difficulty is that the church building now carries a memory of shame. They wonder whether people who once hugged them will avoid them. They may begin editing ordinary conversation, leaving out details of their life, or sitting quietly because speaking honestly feels risky. The wound is not always a loss of faith. Sometimes the wound is not knowing whether faith and belonging can remain together.

Jesus speaks about judgment in Matthew 7, beginning with a warning that reaches directly into our own conduct:

“Judge not, that ye be not judged.”

Matthew 7:1

The words are familiar, and familiarity can cause us to move past them too quickly. Jesus is not asking His followers to abandon discernment or pretend that choices never matter. He is confronting the posture of a person who assumes the right to judge another while refusing to examine themselves. The warning places responsibility upon the one who is speaking, not merely upon the person being spoken about.

Jesus continues by asking why someone is so focused on a small speck in another person's eye while failing to recognize a beam in their own. The image is almost uncomfortable in its exaggeration. A person claiming to help another see clearly may be so blinded by their own condition that their offer of correction becomes absurd. Jesus tells the listener to begin with their own examination. Only then can they see clearly enough to help another person.

“First cast out the beam out of thine own eye; and then shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote out of thy brother’s eye.”

Matthew 7:5

That order matters. Self-examination comes before correction. The passage asks us to consider our motives, our own failures, and the mercy we need. It does not give us permission to use Scripture as a weapon while ignoring the way our words land upon another person.

Consider the difference between two conversations. In one, someone says, “I have already decided what is wrong with you, and I need you to understand my judgment.” In another, someone says, “I have questions, and I want to understand your experience before I speak.” Both conversations may involve deeply held convictions. Only one begins with the humility Jesus describes.

People who have been labeled do not all need the same response. Some need a sincere apology. Some need time away from the person who hurt them. Some want to speak without being interrupted. Others are trying to reconcile a painful church experience with a relationship with God that still matters deeply. We should not prescribe a healing process for someone whose story we have never heard.

Intent matters, but impact matters too. A person may sincerely believe they are helping and still cause harm. Saying “I meant well” does not automatically repair the damage. A willingness to hear, “Those words hurt me,” may be the beginning of a more honest relationship. Such listening does not require abandoning conviction. It requires taking responsibility for the way conviction is communicated.

Perhaps you have been given a label that still echoes in your mind. Perhaps you remember a conversation and wish you could have answered differently. Perhaps you have labeled someone else without knowing the whole story. This chapter is not asking you to pretend those moments never happened. It is asking you to bring them into the light of the teaching Jesus gave.

Before we decide who another person is, we must be willing to examine what we have allowed our words to do. A label can be spoken in seconds and carried for years. The next chapter looks at how a personal judgment can become something larger, shaping an entire congregation's culture and determining who is seen, heard, trusted, or welcomed.
