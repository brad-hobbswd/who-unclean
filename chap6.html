Chapter 6

Who Made You the Gatekeeper?

Spiritual authority is meant to serve. Teaching, pastoral care, accountability, and leadership can help people grow, protect a community, and guide believers through difficult decisions. Authority becomes harmful when personal preference is presented as God's verdict, or when the people entrusted to a leader's care are treated as possessions to manage.

A gatekeeper decides who may enter, who must wait, and who is permitted to approach. The image becomes troubling when someone assumes their position gives control over another person's access to God. Churches have responsibilities and standards. Those responsibilities, however, do not make a human being the owner of God's grace or the final judge of another person's worth.

Jesus told a parable about two men who went to the temple to pray. One spoke with confidence about his own righteousness and compared himself with others. The other asked God for mercy. Jesus' conclusion challenged the man who believed his religious standing placed him above his neighbor.

“God, be merciful to me a sinner.”

Luke 18:13

The publican's prayer is brief, but the posture is different. He does not build his case by listing the people he believes are worse than himself. He recognizes his own need. The parable warns against a kind of religious confidence that depends upon looking down on someone else.

That posture can appear in spiritual leadership. A leader may begin believing that protecting the church means controlling every conversation, deciding whose questions are acceptable, or making people feel they must earn the right to be heard. Such behavior can be disguised as order, concern, or holiness. Yet when leadership produces fear, humiliation, and silence rather than spiritual care, the leader should examine whether authority is being used for the purpose God intended.

Jesus speaks directly about leaders who place burdens on others while refusing to help carry those burdens. He challenges outward appearances that conceal neglect of justice, mercy, and faith. The warning is not about titles alone. It concerns the gap between what a leader demands and what a leader practices.

“They bind heavy burdens and grievous to be borne, and lay them on men's shoulders; but they themselves will not move them with one of their fingers.”

Matthew 23:4

Consider someone who arrives at church after a long absence. They may have rehearsed the courage to walk through the door. Perhaps they were hurt by a former congregation, are uncertain what they believe, or simply need someone to pray with them. A leader's first response can create room for conversation or reinforce the person's fear. A demanding interrogation may make the visitor feel that arrival has placed them on trial. A genuine welcome does not require the leader to have every answer; it requires a willingness to treat the person with respect while learning what they need.

For LGBTQIA+ people, church encounters can become especially painful when spiritual authority makes identity the condition of conversation. Someone may be told their presence is disruptive, their questions are unwelcome, or they must accept a predetermined judgment before being allowed to participate. These experiences should be examined carefully. The church may hold differing theological convictions, but leadership should never be confused with humiliation, intimidation, or the assumption that a person's story does not deserve to be heard.

The ability to influence another person's spiritual life should make us more careful, not less accountable. A teacher may know the words of Scripture and still need to examine their manner. A leader may maintain a position and still need to listen. The authority entrusted to a person is not permission to ignore the effect of their actions.

“Who made you the gatekeeper?” is not a rejection of every form of leadership or accountability. The question concerns the limits of our authority. Who gave us permission to speak as though we know the full extent of God's work in another person's life? Who authorized us to treat our interpretation as the final word on whether someone can seek God, pray, learn, or find community?

Peter's encounter with Cornelius illustrates this distinction. God was already working in Cornelius's life before Peter arrived. Peter's responsibility was to respond to what God had shown him, not claim ownership of the invitation. Spiritual leadership should carry the same humility: recognize where God is working, help people encounter Him, and resist making ourselves the center of the process.

A servant can listen, guide, explain, pray, and offer care without controlling every outcome. This posture does not remove responsibility. It makes responsibility more honest, because the leader must answer for the way people entrusted to their care are treated.

Churches should consider the ordinary ways authority is experienced. Are questions met with patience? Can someone express pain without being accused of attacking the faith? Are boundaries explained with clarity and respect? Does leadership protect people from humiliation, or does humiliation become an accepted way of enforcing expectations?

The people who enter our churches are not problems waiting to be solved, nor are they trophies for a ministry to claim. They are human beings with lives that may be complicated, hopes that may be fragile, and needs that may not be immediately visible. A church can be orderly without being cold, principled without being cruel, and responsible without treating people as though their dignity depends upon a leader's approval.

A gatekeeper may be focused on who should remain outside. A servant is attentive to the people standing in front of them. The difference is revealed in the way we speak, listen, and respond when someone challenges our expectations. The church belongs to Christ, and the authority entrusted to His people should lead toward service rather than control.

The next chapter turns from those who exercise authority to those who have lived with its consequences. Before another argument is made about LGBTQIA+ inclusion, we need to make room for the lives, experiences, and stories of people who have been pushed away. They are not abstractions. They are the people our conversations are about.
