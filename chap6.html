Chapter 6

Who Made You the Gatekeeper?

Some people have become so accustomed to controlling access to religious spaces that they no longer recognize the difference between serving God and managing people. They decide who belongs, who deserves a seat, who may participate, who should remain silent, and whose presence makes others uncomfortable. Over time, the responsibility of welcoming people becomes replaced by the desire to control them.

Jesus never appointed His followers to become owners of His kingdom. He called them servants. The distinction matters, especially when someone begins treating personal authority as though such authority grants permission to determine who may approach God.

The religious leaders of Jesus’ day often questioned the people with whom He associated. Their concerns were not always presented as simple hatred. Many believed they were protecting religious standards, preserving holiness, and maintaining the boundaries of their faith. Yet their desire to protect those boundaries repeatedly placed them in conflict with the One they claimed to serve.

In Luke 18:9–14, Jesus described a Pharisee who stood praying with confidence in his own righteousness while looking down upon another man. His prayer was filled with comparisons. He thanked God that he was not like other people. His religious accomplishments became the foundation for his judgment of someone standing nearby. The publican, meanwhile, could barely lift his eyes toward heaven. He asked God for mercy.

9 And he spake this parable unto certain which trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and despised others:

10 Two men went up into the temple to pray; the one a Pharisee, and the other a publican.

11 The Pharisee stood and prayed thus with himself, God, I thank thee, that I am not as other men are, extortioners, unjust, adulterers, or even as this publican.

12 I fast twice in the week, I give tithes of all that I possess.

13 And the publican, standing afar off, would not lift up so much as his eyes unto heaven, but smote upon his breast, saying, God be merciful to me a sinner.

14 I tell you, this man went down to his house justified rather than the other: for every one that exalteth himself shall be abased; and he that humbleth himself shall be exalted.

Luke 18:9–14

Jesus concluded that the humble man went home justified rather than the man who trusted his own righteousness. The passage reveals how easily religious confidence can become spiritual arrogance. A person may know Scripture, maintain religious practices, and possess a respected position while still misunderstanding the heart of God.

Gatekeeping often begins with the belief that we know enough about another person to determine what God will do with them. A congregation may establish expectations, a leader may make decisions, and a church may have standards governing its ministry. Responsibility and order are not inherently wrong. The danger arises when human authority begins speaking beyond the boundaries God has established, especially when personal opinions are presented as divine verdicts.

Consider someone who walks into a church for the first time after years of feeling unwelcome. They may have spent months gathering courage to attend. They may have prayed in a car before entering the building. They may have rehearsed what to say when someone asks where they have been or why they came. Their arrival may represent a tremendous step toward finding community, faith, or peace.

The first response they receive can shape their understanding of that congregation for years. A cold stare, whispered conversation, unnecessary interrogation, or public humiliation may communicate a message far louder than the sermon. A welcoming smile, genuine conversation, and a willingness to listen can communicate something equally powerful.

LGBTQIA+ individuals have frequently encountered churches where their identity becomes the first subject of conversation rather than their humanity. Questions may be asked before trust has been established. Assumptions may be made before their story is heard. Someone may feel compelled to defend their existence before being allowed to participate in ordinary fellowship.

Such treatment places a tremendous burden upon people who may already have experienced rejection. The church should examine whether its actions reflect the character of Christ or the insecurity of people who feel threatened by unfamiliar experiences. A person should not have to endure humiliation to prove sincerity, nor should compassion be treated as a reward reserved for those who satisfy every expectation.

Jesus confronted religious leaders who burdened people while failing to demonstrate mercy. In Matthew 23:4, 23–28, He spoke against leaders who placed heavy burdens upon others without lifting those burdens themselves. His criticism was directed toward hypocrisy, misplaced priorities, and the misuse of religious influence. He was not impressed by outward displays of authority when the people under that authority were being neglected.

4 For they bind heavy burdens and grievous to be borne, and lay them on men’s shoulders; but they themselves will not move them with one of their fingers.

23 Woe unto you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites! for ye pay tithe of mint and anise and cummin, and have omitted the weightier matters of the law, judgment, mercy, and faith: these ought ye to have done, and not to leave the other undone.

24 Ye blind guides, which strain at a gnat, and swallow a camel.

25 Woe unto you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites! for ye make clean the outside of the cup and of the platter, but within they are full of extortion and excess.

26 Thou blind Pharisee, cleanse first that which is within the cup and platter, that the outside of them may be clean also.

27 Woe unto you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites! for ye are like unto whited sepulchres, which indeed appear beautiful outward, but are within full of dead men’s bones, and of all uncleanness.

28 Even so ye also outwardly appear righteous unto men, but within ye are full of hypocrisy and iniquity.

Matthew 23:4, 23–28

Spiritual leadership requires humility. A pastor, teacher, minister, or church member may influence someone’s understanding of God through a single conversation. Such influence should never be exercised carelessly. The ability to quote Scripture does not eliminate the responsibility to listen. A position of leadership does not make every personal interpretation unquestionable.

Who made you the gatekeeper? Who gave you permission to decide that someone cannot be loved by God, heard by God, or welcomed among His people? These questions are not intended to eliminate accountability or discourage sincere theological discussion. They are intended to remind us that God remains the authority over His kingdom.

Peter learned this lesson when God directed him toward Cornelius. Peter did not create the invitation. Peter did not decide that Gentiles had finally become worthy of hearing the gospel. God was already working before Peter arrived. Peter’s responsibility was to recognize the work of God and respond obediently.

The church must never become so occupied with guarding doors that members fail to recognize the people God is bringing through those doors. Our responsibility is not to compete with the grace of God, nor to make access to His presence dependent upon our approval. We are called to minister, serve, love, teach, and faithfully follow Christ.

A gatekeeper may keep people out. A servant of God should be more concerned with helping people encounter the Lord. The difference is revealed through our actions, especially when someone arrives whose experiences, identity, or questions challenge our assumptions.

The kingdom belongs to God. The church belongs to Christ. The people who walk through our doors are not possessions to control or problems to manage. They are human beings deserving dignity, compassion, honesty, and a willingness to listen. Before we decide who belongs outside, perhaps we should remember who invited us inside in the first place.
