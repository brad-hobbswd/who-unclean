Chapter 6

Who Made You the Gatekeeper?

Spiritual authority is meant to serve. Teaching, pastoral care, accountability, and leadership can help people grow and protect a community. Authority becomes harmful when personal preference is presented as God's verdict, or when the people entrusted to a leader's care are treated as possessions to manage.

A gatekeeper decides who may enter and who must wait. The image becomes troubling when someone assumes that a position gives control over another person's access to God. Churches have responsibilities and standards, yet no human being owns God's grace or knows the full measure of another person's worth.

Jesus told of two men praying in the temple. One compared himself with others and spoke confidently about his own righteousness. The other asked for mercy. Jesus' conclusion challenged the man whose religious standing had become a reason to look down on his neighbor.

The same posture can appear in leadership. A person may call control “order,” silence “protection,” or humiliation “correction.” Consider a visitor returning to church after a painful experience. They may have rehearsed the courage to walk through the door. A probing interrogation can make the visit feel like a trial; a patient welcome can leave room for an honest conversation.

LGBTQIA+ people may encounter spiritual authority through questions that make identity the condition of being heard. Someone may be told their presence is disruptive or their questions are unwelcome. Differing convictions do not excuse intimidation, humiliation, or the refusal to listen to a person's story.

Jesus criticized leaders who placed burdens on others while neglecting justice, mercy, and faith. Leadership is not measured by how much control a person can exert. The ability to influence someone's spiritual life should make us more careful and more accountable.

“Who made you the gatekeeper?” is not a rejection of every form of leadership. The question concerns the limits of our authority. We are called to serve under God, not replace Him. Peter's encounter with Cornelius reminds us that God was already working before Peter arrived. Peter's role was to recognize that work and respond, not to claim ownership of the invitation.

Churches should examine the everyday experience of authority. Are questions met with patience? Can someone express pain without being accused of attacking the faith? Are boundaries explained clearly and respectfully? Does leadership protect people from humiliation, or has humiliation become an accepted way of enforcing expectations?

A servant listens, guides, prays, and cares without needing to control every outcome. The people who enter our churches are human beings with complicated lives and hopes that may be fragile. The next chapter turns toward those who have lived with the consequences of spiritual decisions. Before making another argument about LGBTQIA+ inclusion, we need to hear the people behind the conversation.

I tell you, this man went down to his house justified rather than the other: for every one that exalteth himself shall be abased; and he that humbleth himself shall be exalted.
