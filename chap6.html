Chapter 6

Who Made You the Gatekeeper?

Spiritual authority is meant to serve. Teaching, pastoral care, accountability, and leadership can help people grow, protect a community, and guide believers through difficult decisions. Authority becomes harmful when the person entrusted with responsibility begins treating the people they serve as possessions to manage, or when personal preferences are presented as though God Himself has issued a verdict.

A gatekeeper decides who may enter, who must wait, and who is permitted to approach. The image becomes troubling when someone assumes that their position gives them control over another person's access to God. Churches have responsibilities, and ministry settings may have appropriate standards. Those responsibilities, however, do not make a human being the owner of God's grace or the final judge of another person's worth.

Jesus told a parable about two men who went to the temple to pray. One spoke with confidence about his own righteousness and compared himself with others. The other asked God for mercy. Jesus' conclusion challenged the confidence of the man who believed his religious standing placed him above his neighbor. The story is a warning about what happens when devotion becomes a reason to look down upon another person.

The Pharisee's prayer was full of comparisons. He defined himself by the people he believed he was not. His religious practices became evidence in his own case, while the publican stood nearby as the example against which he measured himself. The problem was not simply that he prayed differently. His confidence had turned into contempt, and he had mistaken his own standing for permission to despise another person.

That same posture can appear in spiritual leadership. A leader may begin believing that protecting the church means controlling every conversation, deciding whose questions are acceptable, or making people feel they must earn the right to be heard. Such behavior can be disguised as order, concern, or holiness. Yet when leadership produces fear, humiliation, and silence rather than spiritual care, the leader should examine whether the authority is being used for the purpose God intended.

Consider a person who arrives at church after a long absence. They may have rehearsed the courage to walk through the door. Perhaps they have been hurt by a former congregation, are uncertain what they believe, or simply need someone to pray with them. A leader's first response can either create room for conversation or reinforce the person's fear. A demanding interrogation may make the visitor feel that their arrival has placed them on trial. A genuine welcome does not require a leader to have every answer; it requires a willingness to treat the person with respect while learning what they need.

For LGBTQIA+ people, church encounters can become especially painful when spiritual authority is used to make identity the condition of conversation. Someone may be told that their questions are unwelcome, that their presence is disruptive, or that they must accept a predetermined judgment before being allowed to participate. These experiences should be examined carefully. The church may hold differing theological convictions, but spiritual leadership should never be confused with humiliation, intimidation, or the assumption that a person's story does not deserve to be heard.

Jesus spoke directly about leaders who placed burdens on others while refusing to help carry those burdens. He criticized religious conduct that focused on outward appearance and neglected justice, mercy, and faith. His words challenge anyone who uses spiritual authority to demand more from others than they are willing to embody themselves.

The passage is a serious reminder that leadership is not measured by how much control a person can exert. It is measured by faithfulness, integrity, and the responsibility to care for people. A teacher may know the words of Scripture and still need to examine their manner. A leader may maintain a position and still need to listen. The ability to influence another person's spiritual life should make us more careful, not less accountable.

The question “Who made you the gatekeeper?” is not a rejection of every form of leadership or accountability. It is a question about the limits of authority. Who gave us permission to speak as though we know the full extent of God's work in another person's life? Who authorized us to treat our interpretation as the final word on whether someone can seek God, pray, learn, or find community? Such questions should remind us that our role is to serve under God's authority, not replace Him.

Peter's encounter with Cornelius illustrates this distinction without requiring us to retell the entire account. God was already working in Cornelius's life before Peter arrived. Peter's responsibility was to respond to what God had shown him, not to claim ownership of the invitation. Spiritual leadership should carry the same humility: recognize where God is working, help people encounter Him, and resist the temptation to make ourselves the center of the process.

A servant does not need to control every outcome. A servant can listen, guide, explain, pray, and offer care while recognizing that God remains the One who calls and transforms. This posture does not remove responsibility. It makes responsibility more honest, because the leader must answer for the way they treat the people entrusted to their care.

Churches should consider the ordinary ways authority is experienced. Are questions met with patience? Can someone express pain without being accused of attacking the faith? Are boundaries explained with clarity and respect? Does leadership protect people from humiliation, or does humiliation become an accepted way of enforcing expectations? The answers reveal more about a congregation's spiritual culture than a title alone ever could.

The people who enter our churches are not problems waiting to be solved, nor are they trophies for a ministry to claim. They are human beings with lives that may be complicated, hopes that may be fragile, and needs that may not be immediately visible. Spiritual leadership should make room for that reality. A church can be orderly without being cold, principled without being cruel, and responsible without treating people as though their dignity depends upon a leader's approval.

A gatekeeper may be focused on who should remain outside. A servant is attentive to the people standing in front of them. The difference is revealed in the way we speak, the way we listen, and the way we respond when someone challenges our expectations. The church belongs to Christ, and the authority entrusted to His people should lead us toward service rather than control.

The next chapter turns from the people who exercise authority to the people who have lived with its consequences. Before another argument is made about LGBTQIA+ inclusion, we need to make room for the lives, experiences, and stories of people who have been pushed away. They are not abstractions. They are the people our conversations are about.
