Chapter 7

LGBTQIA+: The People Religion Has Pushed Away

For many LGBTQIA+ people, church has been more complicated than simply deciding whether to attend. Some grew up singing, praying, serving, and believing. Some found family in a congregation. Others came looking for faith and discovered that the welcome they expected depended on whether they were willing to hide parts of their lives.

There are experiences that do not fit neatly into a paragraph about doctrine. A person may remember a childhood altar, the sound of their mother's singing, a youth group that once felt safe, or a pastor who prayed with them through a difficult season. Later, a conversation about identity or relationships may change how those memories feel. They may not stop loving God, but they may no longer know how to approach the people who taught them about Him.

Imagine someone sitting in a car outside a church on Sunday morning. They have dressed, driven across town, and parked. Through the windshield, they can see people greeting one another at the entrance. They remember the last time a conversation about their identity turned into a public lesson. They wonder whether anyone will ask how they are doing before asking questions they have already heard countless times. After a few minutes, they drive away.

This scenario is illustrative, not a testimony from a specific person. The experience, however, reflects a question many people carry: can I come here as a whole person, or will one part of my life become the only thing anyone sees?

Another person may stay, but learn to carefully manage what they say. They may speak about work but leave out the partner who waited at home. They may share a family story while avoiding the details that make the story honest. They may serve faithfully while wondering whether the people around them would still welcome them if they knew more. The silence can become exhausting, especially when everyone assumes the person is doing well.

People are not symbols in a theological debate. They have jobs, families, friendships, responsibilities, grief, laughter, talents, faith, and ordinary days. Someone may be caring for a parent, raising children, helping a neighbor, supporting a friend through illness, or trying to make sense of their own spiritual life. Their lives deserve to be heard in their own words rather than summarized by someone else's assumptions.

Rejection can leave a person weighing every sentence before speaking. They may decide what to disclose, which relationships to mention, or whether asking for prayer will expose them to judgment. The burden grows when privacy is not respected or when personal information becomes the subject of hallway conversations. A community may never realize how much effort someone spends trying to feel safe.

Listening does not require turning a person's experience into a debate or demanding that they defend their dignity. A conversation can begin with ordinary questions: How have you been? What has been important in your life lately? What are you carrying right now? What kind of support would help? Such questions allow the person to decide what they want to share rather than forcing a disclosure.

Some readers may be revisiting beliefs they were taught. Others may be carrying the hurt of being told they do not belong. Still others may be trying to understand someone they love. A book cannot speak for every experience or resolve every question. The people affected deserve the time, respect, and care that any human being deserves.

For a person who has been pushed away, being heard can be meaningful even when the conversation is difficult. Being listened to does not erase the past, and one compassionate interaction cannot repair every wound. Yet listening can prevent a new wound from being added to the old ones. It can communicate that a person's life is not an inconvenience and their presence does not require an apology.

Before moving to another biblical example, pause here. The people discussed in churches are sitting in our pews, standing outside our doors, and sometimes deciding whether they can return. Their lives are not an interruption to ministry. They are the reason our words and actions matter. The next chapter looks at how Jesus responded to people others had already dismissed, and what those encounters can teach us about our own behavior.
