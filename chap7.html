Chapter 7

LGBTQIA+: The People Religion Has Pushed Away

For many LGBTQIA+ people, church has been more complicated than simply deciding whether to attend. Some grew up singing, praying, serving, and believing. Some found family in a congregation. Others came looking for faith and discovered that the welcome they expected depended on whether they were willing to hide parts of their lives.

Imagine someone sitting in a car outside a church on Sunday morning. They have dressed, driven across town, and parked. Through the windshield, they can see people greeting one another at the entrance. They remember the last time a conversation about their identity turned into a public lesson. They wonder whether anyone will ask how they are doing before asking questions they have already heard countless times. After a few minutes, they drive away.

This scenario is illustrative, not a testimony from a specific person. The experience, however, reflects a question many people carry: can I come here as a whole person, or will one part of my life become the only thing anyone sees?

People are not symbols in a theological debate. They have jobs, families, friendships, responsibilities, grief, laughter, talents, faith, and ordinary days. They may be caring for a parent, raising children, helping a neighbor, serving their community, or trying to make sense of their own spiritual life. Their lives deserve to be heard in their own words rather than summarized by someone else's assumptions.

Rejection can leave a person weighing every sentence before speaking. They may decide what to disclose, which relationships to mention, or whether asking for prayer will expose them to judgment. A community that expects silence may never realize how much effort someone spends trying to feel safe.

Listening does not require turning a person's experience into a debate or demanding that they defend their dignity. A simple conversation can begin with ordinary questions: How have you been? What has been important in your life lately? What would support look like? Such questions allow the person to decide what they want to share.

Some readers may be revisiting beliefs they were taught. Others may be carrying the hurt of being told they do not belong. A book cannot speak for every experience or resolve every question. The people affected deserve the time, respect, and care that any human being deserves.

Before moving to another biblical example, pause here. The people discussed in churches are sitting in our pews, standing outside our doors, and sometimes deciding whether they can return. Their lives are not an interruption to ministry. They are the reason our words and actions matter.
