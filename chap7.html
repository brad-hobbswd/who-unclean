Chapter 7

LGBTQIA+: The People Religion Has Pushed Away

Behind every discussion about LGBTQIA+ people are real human beings with lives that extend far beyond the conversations taking place about them. They are sons and daughters, parents, grandparents, coworkers, neighbors, students, teachers, musicians, and friends. They carry memories, responsibilities, dreams, disappointments, and hopes for the future. Their lives cannot be reduced to a theological debate, a political argument, or a single word spoken from a pulpit.

Many LGBTQIA+ individuals grew up attending church. Some learned Bible verses before they understood the questions they would eventually ask about themselves. They sang in choirs, participated in youth programs, served on committees, attended revivals, prayed at altars, and developed relationships with people they considered family. Their connection to faith may have been genuine and deeply meaningful long before someone told them their identity made them unwelcome.

For some, the difficult journey began with a conversation they never expected to have. A person may have finally found the courage to tell a parent, pastor, or trusted friend something deeply personal, only to discover that the relationship changed almost immediately. The conversation that should have created understanding became an interrogation. Expressions of love were followed by conditions. Private information became the subject of public discussion. The person who had hoped to receive support suddenly found themselves defending their own dignity.

Others never had the opportunity to speak openly. They learned through sermons, jokes, warnings, and conversations that certain people were viewed with suspicion. They watched how the congregation reacted whenever LGBTQIA+ individuals were mentioned. They heard the language used about people whose lives differed from the expectations surrounding them. Long before anyone knew their personal story, they may have begun wondering whether a place they loved would ever accept them.

Religious rejection can be especially painful when faith has been central to a person’s life. Walking away from a congregation may mean losing more than Sunday services. Someone may lose friendships, family gatherings, familiar traditions, opportunities to serve, and the community that helped shape their understanding of God. The loss can leave a person grieving relationships that still exist physically but no longer feel emotionally safe.

Some individuals continue attending church while carrying questions they are afraid to ask. They may worship sincerely while wondering whether the people around them would still welcome them after learning something personal. They may volunteer, pray, give, and participate while quietly trying to determine whether their presence is tolerated or genuinely valued. Living under such uncertainty can become exhausting.

The church should not assume every LGBTQIA+ person has the same experience, the same beliefs, or the same relationship with Christianity. Some remain deeply committed to their faith. Some have found affirming congregations where they can worship without hiding. Some are rebuilding their relationship with God after painful experiences. Others have stepped away from organized religion while still carrying questions about spirituality. Their experiences deserve to be heard without forcing every person into a predetermined narrative.

Listening requires more than waiting for someone to finish speaking so we can explain our position. Listening means allowing a person’s story to challenge the assumptions we have carried. A congregation may believe its treatment of LGBTQIA+ individuals has always been loving, yet the people affected may describe experiences that reveal a different reality. Both the intention and the impact deserve honest examination.

Consider a young adult returning to church after several years away. They arrive quietly, sit near the back, and leave before anyone can ask questions. A familiar member notices them and approaches with a genuine smile. Rather than immediately asking about their relationships, identity, or reasons for leaving, the member simply says, “I am glad you came today. How have you been?” That ordinary moment may accomplish more than a carefully prepared speech. The person is given room to exist without being placed under examination.

Hospitality does not require pretending every theological question has been resolved. Respect does not require abandoning sincere convictions. Compassion does require recognizing that a person’s humanity is never an inconvenience to the conversation. A church can discuss Scripture while refusing to humiliate people. Members can disagree without gossiping, threatening, mocking, or treating someone as though their presence contaminates the congregation.

The words we use matter. Describing an entire group of people as dangerous, deceptive, rebellious, or incapable of genuine faith creates an atmosphere where individuals are judged before they are known. Broad accusations erase personal stories and make meaningful conversations nearly impossible. A person who has been repeatedly condemned may understandably struggle to trust someone who claims to represent the God they have been taught to love.

Jesus repeatedly encountered people whose lives had been discussed by others long before they encountered Him personally (John 4:7–26; Luke 19:1–10; Mark 10:13–16). The conversation with the Samaritan woman shows Jesus crossing a social and religious boundary, while also engaging her honestly about her life and revealing Himself as Messiah. The passage includes their discussion of worship, the woman's questions, and Jesus' declaration of His identity, so the encounter should not be reduced only to a lesson about social acceptance.

John 4:7–26
He was willing to enter conversations, ask questions, recognize faith, and respond to human need. His ministry demonstrated that people were not merely examples in someone else’s religious argument. They were individuals worthy of His attention.

Mark 10:13–16

The church has an opportunity to examine whether LGBTQIA+ individuals are being welcomed as people or approached primarily as subjects requiring correction. A person seeking friendship should not immediately become a project. Someone looking for prayer should not have their private life exposed. A visitor should not be made to feel that every interaction carries an expectation of defending their identity.

Love must be more than a word used to soften rejection. Love should be visible through patience, honesty, kindness, respect, and the willingness to remain present when conversations become difficult. The people who have experienced religious rejection are not asking to become the center of every congregation’s attention. Many simply want the opportunity to worship, develop friendships, contribute to their community, and be treated with the same dignity extended to everyone else.

Before discussing what the church believes about LGBTQIA+ people, perhaps the church should first listen to what LGBTQIA+ people have experienced within the church. Their stories may reveal where compassion has been absent, where assumptions have caused harm, and where opportunities for genuine ministry have been lost.

A person should never have to wonder whether God’s love is being represented by someone who refuses to look them in the eye. The church must be willing to recognize the people standing before them, not merely the arguments surrounding them. Every story deserves to be heard with care, and every human being deserves to be treated with dignity.
