Chapter 7

LGBTQIA+: The People Religion Has Pushed Away

Behind every discussion about LGBTQIA+ people are human beings whose lives cannot be reduced to a theological argument. They are sons and daughters, parents, siblings, coworkers, students, teachers, neighbors, musicians, and friends. They have ordinary responsibilities, inside jokes, favorite songs, family traditions, bills to pay, people they love, and plans for tomorrow. The subject that occupies so much attention in religious conversations is only one part of lives that are full, complicated, and deeply human.

Many LGBTQIA+ people have history with the church. Some grew up there. They learned songs, memorized Scripture, attended youth events, prayed at altars, served in ministries, and built friendships they believed would last. Faith may have been a source of comfort long before questions about identity became difficult. For some, the church was not a place they were trying to enter. The church was home.

Then a conversation happened. Perhaps someone finally trusted a parent, pastor, friend, or ministry leader enough to share something personal. They hoped for honesty and care. Instead, the room changed. Questions came faster than listening. A relationship that once felt secure suddenly seemed conditional. The person left the conversation carrying not only the fear of being misunderstood, but also the uncertainty of whether the people they loved would still want them close.

Sometimes the rejection is direct. A person is told not to return, is removed from a ministry, or is made to feel that their presence will create a problem. Other times the change is quieter. Friends stop calling. Invitations become less frequent. People who once spoke freely become guarded. The person may still be physically present, but the welcome has grown thin enough that leaving begins to feel easier than staying.

A young adult might sit in a parked car outside church for several minutes, trying to decide whether to walk through the doors. The building is familiar. The songs are familiar. Someone inside may still be glad to see them. Yet the memory of a previous conversation makes the entrance feel uncertain. They wonder whether today will be a worship service or another occasion when they have to explain themselves. This is an illustrative scenario, but the hesitation it describes is familiar to people who have experienced rejection in places once considered safe.

Leaving a congregation can mean losing far more than a weekly gathering. Someone may lose friendships, family routines, a place to volunteer, a community that helped during illness or grief, and the spiritual practices that gave structure to life. The grief can be confusing because the people and place may still exist. What has changed is the sense of safety, trust, and belonging.

Some remain in church while learning to hide. They may avoid certain conversations, carefully edit stories about their relationships, or sit quietly when others make comments that feel personal. They may be sincerely worshipping while wondering whether anyone would treat them differently if they knew more. The energy required to remain unnoticed can become exhausting, especially when a person is longing for genuine connection rather than attention.

Not every LGBTQIA+ person has the same story. Some have been supported by family and church. Some have found congregations where they can openly participate and grow. Some hold traditional beliefs about their own lives. Others are affirming and have spent years seeking a church that welcomes them. Some are still sorting through faith, identity, and painful experiences. No single story should be made to represent everyone.

That is why listening matters. Listening does not mean asking someone to disclose more than they wish. It does not mean turning a personal conversation into a public lesson. It means respecting the person enough to let them decide what they want to share, and receiving their words without immediately treating the story as evidence in an argument. A person should not have to perform their pain to convince others that care is needed.

There are questions the church should be willing to hear. What did you experience when you told someone? Were you given time to speak? Did anyone ask what you needed? Were private matters handled with respect? Did the people who disagreed with you continue treating you as a friend, a family member, or a member of the community? Those questions may reveal things that a congregation's good intentions have never noticed.

LGBTQIA+ people should not be expected to carry the entire burden of repairing relationships that have been damaged by rejection. When someone has been spoken about, excluded, humiliated, or abandoned, those responsible for the harm have a part in rebuilding trust. An apology is meaningful when it names what happened without shifting blame. Changed behavior matters because trust is rebuilt through repeated choices, not a single sentence.

A church may have theological questions it continues to study. Those questions should not erase the immediate responsibility to speak with respect, protect privacy, refuse gossip, and treat people with care. The person sitting beside us is not an abstract topic. They are someone whose life continues after the service ends, someone who may remember how we treated them long after we have forgotten the conversation.

Perhaps the first step toward belonging is surprisingly ordinary: learning someone's name, asking how they are, sharing a meal, remembering a detail they told us, or making room for them without making them feel like a project. These small acts do not resolve every theological disagreement. They do tell a person that their presence is not an inconvenience and their humanity is not up for debate.

Before we move into the Gospel stories of people Jesus encountered, we should allow these experiences to remain with us. The people pushed away by religion are not merely illustrations of a lesson. They are living people with memories, faith, questions, relationships, and hopes. Their stories deserve more than a quick response. They deserve to be heard.
