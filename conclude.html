Before You Point Your Finger

Some of the hardest truths to confront are the ones that require us to examine ourselves. We can spend years discussing what someone else believes, how someone else lives, or what someone else should change, while never considering whether our own hearts need attention. We can become so focused on identifying what we believe is wrong in another person that we overlook the responsibility God has placed upon us to walk humbly, love sincerely, and speak carefully.

The question, “Who are you to call unclean what God has made holy?” was never intended to give us permission to ignore Scripture. The question was intended to remind us that God remains the authority over His creation, His people, and His work. Peter learned that his understanding was not the final word. His experience in Acts 10 required him to listen, reconsider, and obey when God revealed something beyond the boundaries he had previously accepted.

Peter’s willingness to respond changed the direction of his ministry. He entered a home he would once have avoided, listened to people he had previously viewed through the limitations of tradition, and witnessed the Holy Ghost working among them. The revelation did not remain confined to a vision. The revelation became visible through Peter’s actions.

That is where this book must leave us. Not merely with something to think about, but with something to examine within ourselves.

How many people have been judged before their stories were heard? How many have been excluded because someone assumed they understood God’s position without considering the full context of Scripture? How many people have walked away from a congregation carrying wounds that could have been prevented through kindness, patience, and a willingness to listen?

Those questions should not be answered hastily. They deserve honest reflection.

The LGBTQIA+ individuals discussed throughout these pages are not theological illustrations without feelings, families, or futures. They are people. Some continue to seek God. Some are rebuilding relationships with faith after painful experiences. Some have found communities where they can worship openly and sincerely. Some are still trying to determine whether the church will ever become a place where they can breathe without fear of rejection.

Their stories should matter to us.

A church may possess a beautiful sanctuary, powerful preaching, organized ministries, and an abundance of biblical knowledge. Yet the way people are treated when they enter that sanctuary remains a testimony of its own. Someone may forget the sermon, but they may never forget the kindness of a person who welcomed them when they felt unwanted. Someone may also remember the humiliation of being treated as though their presence created a problem.

We cannot change every painful experience someone has endured. We can, however, take responsibility for the way we treat people moving forward. We can listen without immediately defending ourselves. We can acknowledge when our words have caused harm. We can stop repeating assumptions about people we have never taken time to know. We can make room for conversations that require patience rather than quick conclusions.

The church does not need to become afraid of difficult questions. Faith should not be threatened by sincere examination. A believer who studies Scripture carefully, considers context, listens to others, and seeks the guidance of God is not abandoning faith. Such a believer is taking the responsibility of understanding seriously.

Our greatest danger may not be the questions someone else asks. Our greatest danger may be believing that we have already answered every question correctly.

The Pharisees were confident in their understanding, yet confidence did not prevent them from missing what God was doing through Jesus. Peter was confident in his convictions, yet God still had something to reveal to him. Both examples remind us that religious familiarity does not guarantee spiritual understanding.

Humility remains necessary.

We should never use the name of God carelessly. We should never speak as though our personal opinions possess the same authority as His Word. We should never make another person’s dignity dependent upon our comfort. We should never confuse protecting our traditions with faithfully following Christ.

The question that began this book still deserves to be heard.

Who are you to call unclean what God has made holy?

Before answering, examine your heart. Before judging someone else’s life, consider the words you have spoken. Before defending your position, ask whether you have truly listened. Before deciding someone does not belong, remember the mercy that brought you into the presence of God.

Perhaps the greatest evidence of spiritual maturity is not the ability to make judgments about everyone around us. Perhaps maturity is demonstrated through the humility to recognize our own need for God’s guidance, the courage to reconsider what we have misunderstood, and the compassion to treat people with dignity even when conversations become difficult.

May we become people who listen before speaking, understand before assuming, and love without making another person feel less human. May our churches become places where Scripture is studied faithfully, questions are approached honestly, and people are treated with the dignity God has given them.

May we never become so occupied with deciding who is unclean that we fail to recognize the work of God standing before us.

And when God challenges our understanding, may we have the courage Peter demonstrated to rise, walk forward, and obey.

The final question is not simply whether we believe we are right.

The final question is whether we are willing to let God correct us.

Before you call someone unclean, make sure you have heard from God.
